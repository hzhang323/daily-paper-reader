# Have an LLM Write Your Anomaly Detector: Autonomous Discovery of Compact, Interpretable Detectors for Time Series 

## David Berghaus 

Lamarr Institute Germany Fraunhofer IAIS Germany david.berghaus@iais.fraunhofer.de 

ABSTRACT 

Time-series anomaly detection trades off predictive accuracy, com-putational efficiency, and interpretability. We use a large language model not as the detector but as the author of one: an autonomous research loop in which the model repeatedly edits a single short NumPy program under a leakage-free objective, keeping the best-scoring detector it finds. The loop discovers two compact detec-tors, one for univariate and one for multivariate series, that de-scribe short windows by their local spectral features and compare them with the training-region distribution through a covariance-aware distance. On the TSB-AD benchmark these detectors lead the field across metrics, ahead of the strongest classical, deep, and foundation-model baselines including Time-RCD, yet they train no network and use no GPU, and the multivariate detector is faster than every similarly performing baseline. LLM-driven program search is thus a practical route to accurate, efficient, and transpar-ent detectors. 

KEYWORDS 

time series anomaly detection, automated algorithm discovery, LLM-driven program search, interpretable models 

1 INTRODUCTION 

Anomaly detection in time series underpins a long list of practi-cal systems, from monitoring industrial machinery and spacecraft telemetry to flagging irregular heartbeats and intrusions in network traffic. Despite sustained attention, the field has not converged on a clear winner. In multivariate settings, deep models can be more competitive, especially when cross-channel structure matters, but strong classical methods such as KMeans and KNN remain among the leading baselines [17, 26]. No single method dominates across datasets, anomaly types, and computational budgets. Classical detectors are cheap and simple but often require dataset-specific preprocessing and threshold tuning to perform well. Deep and foundation models reduce the manual tuning burden but pay for it with training cost, GPU dependence, and opacity. We take a different angle: rather than designing a detector by hand or training a large model, we use an LLM-driven autonomous research loop as a tool to write the detector for us. A language model repeatedly edits a single short Python and NumPy program. It is given the benchmark harness, a strict leakage-free protocol, and an instruction to find an accurate, elegant, single-principle detector. Each edited program is run through the harness and scored on a held-out validation split; this score, together with the whole trajectory of past attempts (including edits that lowered it), informs the next edit. Using a language model as an engine for proposing and refining programs, rather than as the final model, underlies a growing line of work in mathematical and algorithmic discovery [3, 15, 23, 31]. Our question is a practical one: is this tool effective for time-series anomaly detection, and what kind of detector does it produce? As ablations we also hold the harness, the seed program (a simple z-score detector), and the single-principle prompt fixed and vary the search by driving the loop with other models (GPT 5.6 Terra and the smaller open MiniMax-2.5) and comparing against an off-the-shelf evolutionary program-search framework, ShinkaEvolve [15], which is an open-source alternative to AlphaEvolve [23]. The search does not require existing labels: candidates are scored against synthetic anomalies injected into a slice of the training data, never against real labels, so the approach is also applicable to real-world problems where no ground truth exists. At inference the discovered detector is fit per series without labels, under the same operating assumptions as a classical unsupervised detector such as KMeans. We steer the search toward short, efficient programs, and run it separately for univariate and multivariate series. Our contributions are as follows. 

• An LLM that writes the detector. We show that an LLM-driven autonomous research loop is an effective tool for discovering anomaly detectors. The search is straightfor-ward to set up, demands little anomaly-detection expertise from the practitioner, and produces a detector that is com-pact, interpretable, and competitive with the state of the art. Each detector emerged from a single one hour session of roughly 100 edit-and-evaluate iterations. 

• Interpretable, single-CPU programs. Each detector is a short NumPy program with no GPU, no neural training, and no learned weights, so its inductive biases can be read directly from the source and audited. 

• Field-leading accuracy. On the established TSB-AD 

benchmark the two detectors take the most first-place finishes on every reported metric across both regimes, ahead of classical baselines as well as deep and pretrained detectors including the Time-RCD foundation model [14] (Table 2), and also perform competitively on the recent TAB benchmark (Appendix E). arXiv:2610.01223v1 [cs.LG] 1 Oct 2026 Berghaus

2 RELATED WORK 

Benchmarks and evaluation for time series anomaly detection. 

Our primary benchmark is TSB-AD [17], which we evaluate under the 16-family protocol published with Time-RCD [14]. On TSB-AD we compare against detectors spanning pretrained, deep, and classical families: the recent Time-RCD foundation model [14]; the deep reconstruction and forecasting detectors TranAD [38], USAD [2], OmniAnomaly [35], and DCdetector [46], together with the temporal-frequency masked autoencoder TFMAE [7]; and the classical baselines LOF [5], Isolation Forest [16], and subsequence PCA [34]. For a broader picture we additionally report results on the more recent TAB benchmark [26] in Appendix E. 

Autonomous research agents and LLM-driven discovery. A grow-ing body of work uses large language models not as the final model but as an engine for proposing and refining code. FunSearch showed that searching over LLM-proposed programs can yield new results in mathematics [31]; AlphaEvolve broadened this into a general coding agent for scientific and algorithmic discovery [23]; and open frameworks such as ShinkaEvolve study how to make such pro-gram search sample-efficient [15]. Most directly, Karpathy [12] articulates the idea of an autonomous-research loop in a CLI in which an LLM agent repeatedly proposes an edit to a single arti-fact, evaluates it against a fixed metric, and iterates without human intervention. Closest in application, EVIL evolves compact, inter-pretable algorithms for zero-shot inference on event sequences and time series [3]. This program-authoring role is distinct from using a language model as the time-series model: One-Fits-All repurposes a frozen pretrained language-model backbone for downstream tasks including anomaly detection [49], and time-series foundation mod-els score data directly from learned weights [14]. In our setting the language model never sees the data at inference; it only writes the detector program, and the object that scores each series is a few lines of NumPy. 

Synthetic data for foundation models. Synthetic simulation is increasingly used to pretrain foundation models for time series and event sequences, where it supplies a broad prior over mecha-nisms that can be adapted or applied zero-shot to real data. Related time-series foundation-model work uses synthetic anomalies and relative contexts (Time-RCD), causally coherent kernel-generated series (CauKer), or synthetic series paired with symbolic expres-sions (SymTime) [14, 39, 43]. 3 DISCOVERING DETECTORS WITH AN AUTONOMOUS RESEARCH LOOP 

Detector representation. Each candidate detector is a self-contained Python and NumPy function with a fixed signature: it consumes a training segment and a test segment of a series and returns a per-timestep anomaly score. Restricting candidates to short array programs has two benefits. The programs are cheap to run, so a single evaluation across the validation set takes minutes rather than hours, which is what makes an iterative loop practical; and they remain interpretable, so the design choices that perform well can be read directly from the code. 

The loop. We use an autonomous research loop similar to [12]: an LLM-driven agent that edits and re-evaluates a single program with no human intervention. A fixed harness defines the data inter-face, the validation objective, and the scoring code. The language model is allowed to edit only the detector program. In each iter-ation it (i) inspects the current detector program and its recent scores, (ii) proposes a concrete modification, ranging from a hy-perparameter change to a new scoring component or a structural rewrite, (iii) runs the modified program through the harness on the validation split, and (iv) reads the resulting validation score. Unlike a hill-climbing search, the loop applies no automatic accept/reject step: the full trajectory of attempts, including edits that lower the score, stays in the agent’s context and serves as inspiration for later proposals, and the single program returned by the search is the best-scoring one over the whole session (formalized below). We steer the loop toward short, single-principle programs rather than accu-racy alone, which keeps the discovered detectors readable instead of letting them grow into opaque ensembles. We run each search independently for the univariate and multivariate settings and with several driving models (Section 5). The strongest loop, driven by Opus 4.8, discovers related spectral-Gaussian novelty patterns in the two regimes, whereas the other loops and the evolutionary baseline arrive at different programs (Section 6.4). 

Synthetic validation and leakage control. The search uses no real anomaly labels . Candidates are scored by detecting synthetic anom-alies injected only into a held-out slice of each series’ training 

region. The generator is an explicit prior over what an anomaly can look like. We aim for anomalies that are realistic in the mechanism that produces them, like a sensor that freezes, a valid shape appear-ing out of context, a cycle at the wrong period or phase. Its types are taken from the established GutenTAG taxonomy [40], and its mix spans both the magnitude and the in-range shape/contextual families, so that a candidate cannot score well by handling only one of them. The univariate mix emphasizes the in-range families, which cannot be caught from magnitude alone, while the multivari-ate mix also includes joint cross-channel perturbations. Appendix B gives the complete generator and leakage-control protocol. 

4 METHODS DISCOVERED BY THE SEARCHES 

Table 1 summarizes the programs selected by all four searches. The strongest search, the Opus 4.8 loop, found two spectral-Gaussian novelty methods and performed best overall; we therefore describe those two methods below. The other loops produced simpler local-feature methods, while the Shinka evolutions produced ensembles of standard signals. The evaluations can be found in Section 6; constants and complete executable implementations of the Opus methods are in Appendices C and D. The complete, runnable source code of every discovered detector (all four searches, in both the univariate and multivariate regimes) is available in an anonymized repository. 1

The Opus methods. The Opus loop was run once for each regime, and both times it arrived at a detector built on the same idea. Each one slides a short window along the series, describes the rhythms inside that window, and then asks how unusual that description looks next to normal stretches of the training data. Windows that stand out get a high anomaly score, which is turned back into a per-timestamp score. Both detectors are fast, with a running time that grows roughly in proportion to the length of the series. Where they differ is the description they use: the univariate detector captures how a window’s energy is spread across its rhythms, while the multivariate detector captures the combined rhythms of all channels at once. 

The univariate detector. Rather than looking at the raw values in a window, this detector looks at how the window’s energy is split between fast and slow rhythms, and at the overall shape of that split. It turns each window into a short list of descriptive numbers, for example how peaked or how flat the spectrum is, where its energy sits, and how much of it falls in slow versus fast rhythms, together with a term that reacts when part of the window suddenly goes quiet or freezes. Earlier frequency-based detectors compare a spectrum against a smoothed baseline [30], score windows by their distance to a nearest neighbour [47], or learn a frequency-domain reconstruction [7, 45]; what is different here is that the description is fixed, easy to read, and judged as a whole rather than one number at a time. The score therefore rises not just when a single number is unusual, but when the normal relationship between them breaks. Because the description is mostly about shape and rhythm rather than the raw size of the values, the detector also catches anomalies whose values stay in the normal range and that value-based de-tectors tend to miss, such as cycles running at the wrong period or phase, out-of-context subsequences, and frozen segments. It re-peats this at several window sizes and blends the results, giving extra weight to the windows that look genuinely improbable. To our knowledge this particular construction has not previously been used for anomaly detection, and we verify empirically that it out-performs the closest existing frequency-domain and feature-based detectors (Section 6.2). The multivariate detector. For series with many channels, the detector describes the rhythms of all channels together. It slides a window across the channels, removes each window’s level so that only its shape matters, and measures how much energy each channel carries in a few frequency bands. Stacking these across all channels gives one description of the window’s joint rhythm. It then builds a single statistical model of what normal windows look like and scores each window by how far it lies from that model. Because all channels share one model, the score reacts not only when a single channel’s rhythm changes, but when the usual relationship between the channels breaks, even if every channel on its own still looks perfectly normal. To stay fast, the detector samples windows at regular strides and caps how many it uses, so its cost grows roughly in proportion to the length of the series even for very long, high-dimensional data. And because the model has a fixed size rather than growing with the data, one fit on a short training segment carries over to much longer test series. The full description and all constants are given in Appendix C (Eqs. 5–6). 

5 EXPERIMENTAL SETUP 

Benchmarks and datasets. Our primary evaluation is the estab-lished TSB-AD [17], using the 16-family protocol of Time-RCD [14]: eleven univariate families (IOPS, MGAB, NAB, NEK, Power, SED, Stock, TODS, UCR, WSD, Yahoo) and five multivariate families (MSL, PSM, SMAP, SMD, SWaT), all listed in Table 2. For com-pleteness we additionally evaluate on TAB [26], a more recent benchmark that assembles a larger and differently curated field, both univariate (14 dataset families, 816 series) and multivariate (25 families, 214 series), under a single evaluation pipeline and reports a wider panel of range- and volume-aware metrics. We report its full fields in Appendix E. 

Metrics. On TSB-AD we follow the Time-RCD protocol and re-port the affiliation F-measure [11], a tolerance-aware temporal F1 (F1-T), the point-wise standard F1, and VUS-PR [24]. On TAB we report the panel it standardizes: the volume-under-the-surface pair (VUS-PR, VUS-ROC), the two range-aware areas under the curve (R-AUC-PR, R-AUC-ROC) [24], and the affiliation F-measure [11]. Precise definitions are in Appendix A. 

Baselines and protocol. On TSB-AD we adopt the published 16-family protocol of Time-RCD [14] and place our detectors, scored under that protocol, against the baseline results reported for it: the Time-RCD foundation model [14], the deep detectors TranAD [38], USAD [2], OmniAnomaly [35], DCdetector [46], and TFMAE [7], and the classical baselines LOF [5], Isolation Forest [16], and subsequence PCA [34]. Time-RCD is the strongest non-classical baseline in this field. On TAB (Appendix E) we compare against the full field of detectors bundled with the benchmark, spanning non-learning, classical, and deep methods (for example HBOS [8], EIF [9], KMeans, DWT-MLEAD [37], and deep models such as TimesNet [41], ModernTCN [20], PatchTST [22], iTransformer [18], CATCH [42], DUET [27], and the Anomaly Transformer [44]), together with the foundation models Time-RCD [14], TSPulse [6], and Chronos [1] (the last scored by its one-step forecast residuals, univariate only), all run from their released checkpoints without tuning. We verified that our evaluation scripts reproduced the numbers reported in the paper, confirming the wrappers are faithful and that the comparison is one-to-one. 

The searches we compare. As ablations we ask how much the discovered detector depends on the driving model and on the search procedure, rather than on the harness alone. We compare four searches that hold the harness, the seed program, the synthetic-validation objective, and the single-principle prompt fixed , varying only the model and the search procedure. Three are instances of the agentic loop above, driven by various language models (Claude Opus 4.8, GPT 5.6 Terra, and the smaller open MiniMax-2.5), each run at the model’s default reasoning-effort setting for a single one-hour session of roughly 100 evolutions per regime. The fourth swaps the agentic loop for ShinkaEvolve [15], an off-the-shelf evolutionary program-search framework (island populations with an elite archive) in the style of AlphaEvolve [23], driven by the same open MiniMax-2.5 model for a matched budget of 100 generations per regime. For every search we select the program with the best synthetic-validation score and evaluate it on both benchmarks exactly as we evaluate the baselines. 6 RESULTS 6.1 Accuracy on TSB-AD 

Table 2 reports accuracy on TSB-AD under the Time-RCD protocol, across the eleven univariate and five multivariate dataset families and the four protocol metrics (affiliation F-measure, temporal F1-T, standard F1, and VUS-PR). The Opus 4.8 Loop row applies the univariate detector to the univariate families and the multivariate detector to the multivariate families; within each metric the best value per family is marked with a podium medal ( 1 2 3 for first, second, and third), and the final three columns count how often each method places first, second, or third across the sixteen families. 

The Opus loop leads the field. The detector discovered by the Opus 4.8 loop takes the most first-place finishes on every one of the four metrics, and its advantage is widest on the precision-oriented met-rics (standard F1 and VUS-PR). Its closest competitor is Time-RCD, a foundation model pretrained for zero-shot anomaly detection. The remaining deep detectors and the classical baselines trail fur-ther back and top only isolated families. Among our own searches the one exception is the multivariate side of TSB-AD, where the GPT 5.6 Terra loop’s detector is marginally ahead of the Opus one (Section 6.4, Table 7). The Opus detector remains clearly in front on the univariate families and on both halves of TAB (Appendix E, Tables 5 and 6). 

Corroboration on TAB. The larger TAB field (Appendix E, Tables 5 and 6) points the same way, with some nuance. On multivariate TAB the Opus loop’s detector is first on all four range-aware metrics, ahead of every classical and deep baseline. On univariate TAB it leads the ranking-oriented ROC metrics, while KMeans is strongest on the precision metrics, so the two share the top of that field. On the region-based affiliation F-measure the detector is only mid-field on TAB, a consequence of emitting sharp novelty peaks rather than the broad predicted regions that this measure rewards (Section 7). 

6.2 Did the loop rediscover an existing detector? 

The discovered detectors describe each window by a fixed feature vector and score it by its distance to a model of normal windows, a design that overlaps with two established families: frequency-domain detectors and feature-based subsequence detectors. It is therefore fair to ask whether the loop merely rediscovered a method that already exists. To test this directly, we compare against the pub-lished methods closest to this design under the identical protocol, each run from its released implementation (Table 3). Two are canon-ical frequency-domain detectors: spectral-residual saliency [30] and a Fourier-reconstruction detector [29]. The others build on catch22 [19], the canonical interpretable time-series feature set, which we use in two forms. The first is C22MP [36], which scores catch22 features by a matrix profile. The second, our sharpest con-trol, pairs the catch22 features with our own novelty framework: they are scored by the same Mahalanobis distance to a Gaussian of normal windows (“catch22 + Mahalanobis”). The discovered detector leads all four metrics, and by a wide margin on the precision-oriented VUS-PR (53 .9 versus at most 35 .0). The search procedure matters less, but shapes what gets built. Fix-ing the model to MiniMax-2.5 and varying only the search pro-cedure (the agentic loop versus the ShinkaEvolve evolutionary search) gives no consistent winner on accuracy: neither is reliably ahead of the other across the two benchmarks and the two regimes (Table 7 and Appendix E), so the search structure looks like a smaller factor than the driving model. Where the two procedures pull apart is in the kind of program they return. The agentic loops stayed close to the single-principle steer and produced shorter programs built mainly around one idea: the Opus loop returned two compact, genuinely novel single-principle detectors (Section 4), and even the other loops kept to a single idea, as with the GPT loop’s uni-variate detector that scores the local total variation of a window in training-percentile coordinates, though those programs transferred to the held-out field far less well, most starkly on univariate. The ShinkaEvolve search, under the identical prompt asking for one novel principle and explicitly discouraging the fusion of standard methods, instead converged in both regimes on a hacky weighted ensemble of standard methods: several standard signals in the uni-variate program (autocorrelation structure, gradient-direction en-tropy, a local distribution-divergence statistic, a local-rank score, and the original z-score signal) and, in the multivariate one, a mix that includes a Mahalanobis distance, per-channel residuals from predicting each channel from the others, the z-score, and velocity and acceleration terms. We suspect this might be caused by how the framework works: the evolutionary framework often asks to merge different ideas together, and (by construction) it also presents diverse programs as context at each evolution step, so incremental additions to an already competitive program tend to be kept even when they grow a larger, less coherent ensemble rather than a fresh single principle. A single run per regime cannot pin down that mechanism, but it is a plausible reason the shared prompt did not prevent this kind of heuristic accretion. The evolutionary programs also end up markedly longer and more heavily hand-tuned than the loops’, the evolutionary search is much slower to run at several hours per regime against the loops’ roughly one-hour sessions, and its multivariate ensemble is comparatively slow at inference on the longest series, unlike the tight runtime budget the loop’s detectors respect. 

7 DISCUSSION AND LIMITATIONS 

The appeal of letting an agent discover detectors lies not only in the measured numbers but in the resulting artifact: each detector is a short, readable program, so its inductive biases are inspectable and its cost is predictable. This contrasts with opaque trained models and makes the method easy to audit and to port. It also changes where human effort goes, from designing the detector to designing the harness, the synthetic-anomaly generator, and the objective that the agent optimizes against. Several caveats apply. First, the detector’s standing depends on the metric family and the benchmark. On TSB-AD it leads across the reported metrics. On TAB it dominates the multivariate side but shares the top of the univariate side with KMeans, which is ahead on the precision metrics there, and it is only mid-field on the region-based affiliation measure because it is a sharp scorer rather than a region predictor. Second, although we evaluate on two independently curated benchmarks, a still broader study across more benchmarks and dataset families (and potentially real-world deployment) would be needed to confirm the generality of the discovered detectors. Third, the synthetic generator is where prior knowledge about anomalies enters. We tried to keep it broad and realistic but certain types of anomalies that are not captured well by the prior might be missed by the discovered detectors. The generator is also a design choice that could be varied, and we do not claim that the discovered detectors are optimal for every conceivable anomaly type. Finally, the discovery has a one-time cost in language-model queries and compute; our efficiency claims concern the discovered detector at inference time, not the search that produced it. Though we do report that the searches could be performed within the 20 USD subscription plans and should therefore be accessible to many researchers and practitioners. 

8 CONCLUSION 

We asked whether an LLM-driven autonomous research loop is an effective tool for writing time-series anomaly detectors. Driven by frontier models and steered toward short, single-principle programs, the loop discovered related spectral-Gaussian novelty detectors in the two regimes: a rich, multi-scale spectral-organization signature for univariate series, and a joint per-channel band-power model for multivariate series. On the TSB-AD benchmark these detectors lead the reported metrics and remain near the top of the broader TAB field. Both are fully unsupervised, run on a single CPU core with no GPU and no neural training, and consist of short, readable code that keeps them cheap and auditable. Berghaus

A METRIC DEFINITIONS 

We summarize the standardized metrics used in the main text; full definitions are given by the works we cite. All accuracy metrics lie in [0, 1] and are higher-is-better. 

Binarization. Our detectors output only raw per-timestamp scores 𝑎 𝑡 ; we never threshold them ourselves. The region- and point-based measures that need a binary prediction obtain it inside the evaluation harness, identically for every method (ours and the baselines): under the Time-RCD/TSB-AD protocol of the main text the harness reports each threshold-dependent metric at its score-optimal operating point (sweeping the score quantile and keeping the best attainable value), while on TAB the label metrics are averaged over a fixed grid of contamination ratios. Threshold-free measures (the AUC and VUS families) use the raw scores 𝑎 𝑡 directly. 

Range-aware AUCs (R-AUC-PR, R-AUC-ROC). Standard AUC-PR and AUC-ROC treat every timestamp independently and with equal weight, which understates the quality of detectors on extended (subsequence) anomalies. The range-aware variants add a tolerance region around each labeled anomaly so that scores raised near a true event are credited, and then integrate precision-recall or ROC behavior over that construction [24]. 

Volume under the surface (VUS-PR, VUS-ROC). VUS removes the dependence on a single tolerance width by sweeping the size of the boundary buffer and integrating the resulting family of PR or ROC curves into a volume [24]. This yields a threshold-free and buffer-width-free summary that is well suited to subsequence anomalies, and it is the headline metric on the TSB-AD leaderboard [17]. We order the TAB baseline field by VUS-PR for this reason (listing our discovered detectors first), while reporting the full panel of metrics rather than any composite of them. 

Affiliation F-measure (Aff-F). The affiliation metric is a parameter-free, label-based measure that pairs predicted anomaly events with ground-truth events and scores their temporal affiliation, combining an affiliation precision and recall into an F-measure [11]. It applies uniformly to point and subsequence anomalies. Because it scores predicted regions , threshold-free scorers like ours are evaluated on the binary regions the harness derives from the raw scores as described above. 

Point-wise and temporal F1 (Standard-F1, F1-T). The standard F1 is the point-wise F-measure between the binarized predictions ˆ 𝑦 

and the ground-truth labels 𝑦 , computed timestamp by timestamp. Writing TP, FP, and FN for the timestamp-wise true positives, false positives, and false negatives, it is the harmonic mean of precision and recall, F1 = 2 TP 2 TP + FP + FN =   12

 Prec −1 + Rec −1  −1 , (2) with Prec = TP /( TP + FP ) and Rec = TP /( TP + FN ). The temporal F1-T relaxes this with a per-series tolerance around each labeled event, so a prediction close to a true anomaly is credited without requiring exact timestamp overlap. Both are reported under the Time-RCD protocol on TSB-AD [14], with the binary predictions ˆ 𝑦

B SEARCH CONFIGURATION 

Synthetic anomaly generator. The validation slice is the last 30% of each series’ training region (at least 128 steps); synthetic anom-alies are injected only into this slice with a fixed seed. Anomalies are placed as non-overlapping windows, sampled until a target fraction of the slice is anomalous (subject to a minimum number of windows and a minimum gap between them), with each win-dow’s length drawn uniformly from a fixed range. Each window receives a single anomaly type, drawn uniformly from a mix that deliberately spans both magnitude and in-range shape/contextual 

families, so that a competitive detector must handle both; the types reimplement those in the GutenTAG generator [40]. Each type is intended to imitate a generic mechanism that occurs in prac-tice: a frozen sensor, a valid shape out of context, a stretched or misaligned cycle, a burst of noise, a drifting level. Magnitudes are expressed in units of the channel’s robust standard deviation 𝜎 : a 

mean shift adds U ( 0.5, 4) 𝜎 with random sign; a platform sets the window to its median + U (− 3, 3) 𝜎 ; a trend adds a linear ramp of total rise up to U ( 0.5, 4) 𝜎 ; a variance burst adds Gaussian noise of scale U ( 1.5, 6) 𝜎 ; an extremum is a single-point spike of U ( 6, 40 ) 𝜎 ;an amplitude anomaly rescales the window’s oscillation about its mean by U ( 1.5, 3) (or its reciprocal) under a smooth bell envelope; a discord splices in a real subsequence copied from a distant part Berghaus 

of the same series, level-aligned to the local median (a real but out-of-context shape); a warp resamples a neighboring stretch into the window (a stretched/compressed, wrong-period cycle); a phase 

shift circularly rotates the window by a large fraction of its length (a misaligned cycle); a flatline freezes the window at its starting value (a frozen sensor); and a scramble randomly permutes the win-dow’s samples in time (its value distribution is unchanged but its local temporal structure is destroyed). All types except the point extremum label the entire window; the extremum labels only the spike timestamp. The two regimes use the following generator con-figurations, which are the ones used for every experiment in the paper.

C DISCOVERED DETECTOR CONFIGURATIONS 

We give the key constants present in the discovered programs; complete executable implementations appear in Appendix D. Both detectors standardize from training-region statistics, slide a window, derive local spectral features, and score their novelty under a Gauss-ian model of normal training windows. The univariate detector uses robust median/MAD feature scaling and a square-root Mahalanobis score; the multivariate detector uses per-channel mean/std scaling and a squared Mahalanobis score. 

Univariate detector. The window is Hann-tapered; its descrip-tor concatenates the spectral-organization signature (spectral en-tropy, log spectral flatness, spectral centroid, peak concentration, log energy, log-power/log-frequency slope, low/high band ratio), a within-window complexity-collapse term (least-active sub-block variation vs whole-window variation), and a bank of log-spaced band-powers. The descriptor enters one shrunk full-covariance (0 .95 full-covariance weight, shrunk toward a scaled identity) Ma-halanobis distance (Eq. 3), so each coordinate is scored conditional on the others.
