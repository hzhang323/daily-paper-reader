# EXPLAINING TIME SERIES FORECASTING WITH HORIZON -R ESOLVED ATTRIBUTION 

Seunghan Lee, Jun Seo, Jaehoon Lee, Junhyeok Kang, Sangjun Han, Sungdong Yoo, Minjae Kim, Tae Yoon Lim, Dongwan Kang, Hwanil Choi, Soonyoung Lee, Wonbin Ahn 

LG AI Research 

## ABSTRACT 

Recent advances in explaining time series (TS) models have produced methods that identify which past values a prediction depends on. However, most existing methods return a single 

importance vector, assuming that every predicted step depends on the same past values. In this paper, we show that this assumption does not hold, as different forecast steps depend on dif-ferent past values . Motivated by this observation, we propose Horizon-Resolved e Xplanation (HRX ), which adds a horizon axis to the explanation, so that every forecast step receives its own importance map. HRX is a simple yet effective plug-in framework with three com-ponents: 1) an estimator that reads these maps out of any differentiable forecaster without modifying the TS backbone, 2) an evaluation protocol that validates the horizon axis by measuring how much a single forecast step changes when the inputs an importance map ranks highest are removed, and 3) a rank criterion that predicts in advance whether the axis is worth resolving on a given TS. We further show that this step-wise dependence is low-dimensional ,as the explanations of all steps are built from a few shared maps whose number does not grow with the forecast length. Extensive experiments across various backbones and datasets show that the improvement comes from the horizon axis and holds for estimators of previous explanation methods. Code is available at https://github.com/seunghan96/HRX .

## 1 INTRODUCTION 

Time series (TS) forecasting is widely used in various fields, including finance (Lee et al., 2026a;c) and traffic (Liu et al., 2024a). A range of TS forecasting methods have been developed based on different architectures, such as Transformers (Liu et al., 2024a), multi-layer perceptrons (MLPs) (Zeng et al., 2023; Lee et al., 2024), and convolutional networks (Wu et al., 2023). In parallel, TS explanation methods ask why a model produces a particular output (Queen et al., 2023). However, existing TS explanation methods return a single explanation vector e ∈ RL for the entire output, whether that output is a class label (classification) or an entire forecast horizon (forecasting). A forecast, unlike a class label, is a trajectory of H values, and a single vector collapses it into one summary. This implicitly assumes that every future value depends on the same past values.

We further propose an evaluation protocol that scores one forecast step at a time, which no prior metric can do. We also propose a rank criterion, the rank of E, that says in advance whether the axis brings any gain over the shared vector on a given TS. The main contributions are summarized as: • We show that TS forecasters read different parts of the lookback window for different forecast steps, and that the single explanation vector returned by prior works cannot express this structure. • We propose Horizon-Resolved e Xplanation ( HRX ), a simple yet effective plug-in framework that resolves the explanation along the horizon axis, so that the explanation becomes a matrix with one row per forecast step. HRX is applicable to the estimator of any explanation method and to any differentiable forecaster, and needs no change to the TS backbone or auxiliary training. • We propose an evaluation protocol, since established TS explanation metrics score one label per input and therefore cannot score a single forecast step. It measures how much one step degrades when we delete what its own row of E marks rather than what the shared vector marks. • We show that the horizon axis is low-dimensional, as keeping only the leading directions of E

recovers the full gain while a single direction recovers none of it. We turn the rank of E into a criterion that predicts the gain from the matrix alone, without running the protocol. • We conduct extensive experiments over 56K runs across 26 backbones and 15 benchmarks, and show that the improvement comes from the horizon axis rather than any particular estimator. The axis also holds under the explanation metrics of prior works and against known ground truth. 

## 2 RELATED WORKS 

Three lines of related work (Appendix P.1). Work related to ours falls into three lines: • TS forecasting models (Nie et al., 2023; Liu et al., 2024a). • Gradient-based explanation (Simonyan et al., 2013; Sundararajan et al., 2017). • Perturbation-based explanation (Crabb´ e & van der Schaar, 2021; Enguehard, 2023). The first is what we attach to, since our framework treats any forecaster as a black box and changes nothing inside it. ## 3 METHODOLOGY 

The framework has four parts: (Section 3.1) what an explanation should be, (Section 3.2) how we compute it, (Section 3.3) how we validate it, and (Section 3.4) how many directions it needs. • Explanation matrix. From one shared vector to a matrix with one row per forecast step. • Estimator. The input gradient of one forecast step fills a row, and any estimator can replace it. • Evaluation protocol. A test of whether a row explains its own step better than another row. • Rank criterion. The singular values of E say how many of the H rows are effectively distinct. 3.1 EXPLANATION MATRIX

Efficient implementation: Batched backward calls. A backward call propagates from a single scalar, so a naive implementation needs one pass per forecast step and H passes in total. To fill several rows in one call, we replicate the input, give each replica a different h, and differentiate the sum of the selected outputs, which keeps the rows separate because each replica is its own leaf in the graph . The cost of the whole matrix then stays within a small multiple of a forward pass. 3.3 EVALUATION PROTOCOL

## 4 EXPERIMENTS 

Datasets. We evaluate on 15 benchmarks grouped into two domains. The first is a widely used general-domain suite, which is ETTh1, ETTh2, ETTm1, ETTm2, Electricity, Exchange, PEMS03, PEMS04, PEMS07, PEMS08, Solar, Traffic, and Weather (Wu et al., 2021). The second is a new financial benchmark we assemble from the daily closing prices of the 100 largest listed US compa-nies between 2018 and 2025 (Aroussi, 2024). We add the financial domain, where decisions have to be justified to auditors and regulators (Yeo et al., 2025). Details are discussed in Appendix A. 

Backbones. We build on the Time-Series-Library (Wang et al., 2024b) and evaluate 26 backbones spanning linear, convolutional, recurrent, and Transformer families. We add three deliberately mini-mal architectures, which are a single linear map, a small CNN, and a vanilla Transformer, so that the phenomenon can be separated from any modern design choice. Details are discussed in Appendix A. 

Experimental setup. To test the horizon axis where it would have to hold in practice, we sweep the lookback length, the forecast length, and the depth over the ranges the forecasting literature uses, and we run every configuration with three random seeds. Metrics. We also report metrics of prior works, which are 1) AUPRC, AUROC, AUP and AUR on the synthetic data, where we know the ground truth, and 2) Comprehensiveness, AOPC, Sufficiency and Insertion on the real-world data, with details discussed in Appendix F.3. 4.1 SANITY CHECK ON SYNTHETIC DATASET 

Why this check comes first. On real data, we never know which past values a forecast step actually depends on. We therefore cannot tell whether our method reports the truth or only an artifact of the metric. To find out, we build six kinds of synthetic dataset in which we decide that dependence ourselves and check what our method returns, which we assess both qualitatively and quantitatively. 

Synthetic datasets. The target of each generator is an explicit function of a few chosen lookback positions, so the dependence is fixed by how we build the data rather than asserted afterwards. Note that ∂y h/∂x t then follows from the definition and is exactly zero at every position we did not choose. Each generator takes the same form yh = w(h) · xS(h), where S(h) is the small set of lookback positions that step h reads and w(h) is a unit-norm weight vector that varies smoothly with 

h. They differ only in how S(h) moves with h, and Appendix G defines all 6 of them. 

Quantitative results. Table 3 reports the performance over the six synthetic datasets (generators) with three basic backbones, where every cell is a median over 54 runs, namely 6 generators, 3 window shapes, and 3 seeds. The explanation matrix beats the shared vector on all four ground-truth metrics and all three backbones. On our proposed metrics, all three backbones show the ideal direction, gown > 0, gshuffled < 0, and gmargin > g own . Note that these three are exactly zero for prior works, since they score one forecast step at a time and the shared vector has no per-step value. 

Qualitative results. To validate that the matrix recovers the true dependence, we compare the positions that actually generated each forecast step with what each method returns on the same trained backbone (Figure 4). Prior works repeat the shared vector down the horizon, which flattens the structure the generator planted into vertical stripes. ## 6 CONCLUSION 

We showed that TS forecasters read different parts of the lookback window for different forecast steps , and we proposed horizon-resolved explanation, which recovers it as a matrix without modify-ing the TS backbone, together with a protocol that isolates the horizon axis. Reading an input gradient out of a TS backbone meets two difficulties, and one fails loudly 

while the other fails quietly . The first is that many backbones normalize their input in place, as in x /= stdev , which autograd refuses to differentiate because the overwritten tensor is the one it saved for the backward pass. We therefore rewrite these operations out of place with a 18 Preprint 

TorchFunctionMode , and assert that the forward output is identical before and after, since code that relies on the side effect of an in-place call would otherwise become a no-op without any sign. The second is that a network can genuinely ignore its input, as we found one backbone whose decoder outputs a constant at initialization, which makes both the gradient and the deletion effect exactly zero. Note that such a zero is a fact about the untrained model rather than a finding about forecasting, so we validate the estimator on trained models only. E.3 PORTING THE ESTIMATORS TO FORECASTING 

Every estimator of Section 4.3 is reimplemented from the definition in its own paper rather than taken from a library, since this puts all of them on the same forward interface, the same perturbation operator, and the same protocol. The quantity each one is asked to preserve is the only thing we change, and it becomes ˆy[h] in place of the scalar the original method reads. 

Gradient methods. Saliency, gradient × input, and integrated gradients differentiate ˆy[h] instead of a class score, and integrated gradients uses the mean of the lookback window as its baseline. Occlusion corrupts one window of the lookback at a time and reads the change at step h alone, rather than the change in a class score. 

Mask methods. Dynamask, ExtremalMask, and ContraLSP were published for time series, but their objective reduces the output to a scalar. We keep their objective and let it preserve ˆy[h] under the same budget on the mask, so the axis makes each of them solve one optimization problem per forecast step. Note that the same code with the axis removed preserves the whole forecast at once, which is the form closest to the original papers and the one scored in Appendix K.3.

The two naive baselines. The baselines are predicting the standardized target as zero and extending the last observation, and a run counts as skilled only when its test error beats both. ## L LIMITATIONS IN DETAIL 

We cannot predict the effect size. A practitioner cannot tell in advance how much the axis will buy on a new series. We checked spectral concentration, autocorrelation, forecast-target correlation across steps, and naive-forecast difficulty, and no candidate reached |r| = 0 .07 on real data. An early analysis suggested that the number of stacked periods drives the gain, but that generator scaled the target variance with the number of periods. The correlation is -0.169 once the scale is fixed, so we report the failure rather than the artifact. 

We found no downstream utility. None of the uses we tried turned the matrix into a better forecast or a better diagnosis. Neither explanation-guided input selection nor horizon-specific pruning gave a gain distinguishable from zero, which means HRX is a measurement tool and nothing more. 

Two properties of our estimator. Our default estimator inherits the known weaknesses of input gradients, and our evidence on real data is faithfulness-based, which is why we anchor our method on constructed labels first. The effective rank reads |E| and therefore measures how many components the magnitude map is built from, not how many the signed Jacobian is. Structure in the signs alone is invisible to it, which is why the calibration of Section 4.1 plants the rank in the magnitudes. 

## M THE RANK OF THE EXPLANATION MATRIX 

What the effective rank counts. Eq. 6 is the participation ratio of the singular values of E, which counts how many directions carry the magnitude of the matrix rather than how many are nonzero. A matrix whose rows are positive multiples of one vector has a participation ratio of one, and every quantity of Table 2 is then pinned to zero, because the deletion order of Section 3.3 depends on a row only through the order it induces. We compute the ratio on |E| for the same reason the protocol does, which is that the order a row induces does not see the sign. Structure that lives in the signs alone is therefore invisible to the ratio, which is why the synthetic generators of Section 4.1 plant it in the magnitudes.

## N DISTANCE BETWEEN FORECAST STEPS 

Why a distance is needed. The protocol of Section 3.3 draws a permutation π uniformly over the horizon, which validates that a row belongs to its own step but does not say how far apart two steps have to be before their rows stop agreeing. A matrix whose rows vary smoothly answers that question differently at the two ends, since a uniform permutation lands far from a step far more often than it lands next to it. We therefore replace π with a fixed distance ∆ and score step h with the row 

E[h + ∆] , falling back to E[h − ∆] at the ends of the horizon and averaging the two where both exist. Table 20 sweeps ∆ and puts the uniform permutation of the protocol on the same axis. ## P RELATED WORKS 

P.1 LINES THIS WORK SITS BESIDE 

Section 2 names the three lines this work sits beside, and this section gives each of them in full. 

TS forecasting models. Architectures for long-horizon TS forecasting have settled into a few fam-ilies. Transformer variants reshape attention along the sequence axis (Zhou et al., 2021; Wu et al., 2021; Zhou et al., 2022b; Nie et al., 2023; Liu et al., 2024a), linear and MLP designs argue that most of the accuracy follows from trend and seasonality structure rather than from attention (Zeng et al., 2023; Das et al., 2023; Wang et al., 2024a), and convolutional designs fold the series along its periods so that a 2D kernel can read them (Wu et al., 2023). Our framework treats any of them as a black box it attaches to, and details of the 26 backbones we explain are deferred to Appendix A. 

Gradient-based methods. These methods read importance from the derivative of the output with respect to the input , so a single backward pass scores every input value. Saliency (Simonyan et al., 2013) uses the magnitude of the gradient and gradient × input multiplies it by the input value at each position, while integrated gradients (Sundararajan et al., 2017) integrates it along a path from a baseline and SmoothGrad (Smilkov et al., 2017) averages it over noisy copies of the input. Note that none of them is optimized for a given input, which is what keeps this family cheap. These methods transfer directly to TS, where naive transfer is shown to conflate the time and feature axes and a two-stage rescaling is proposed (Ismail et al., 2020). 

Perturbation-based methods. These methods change the input and read importance from how much the output moves . Dynamask (Crabb´ e & van der Schaar, 2021) learns a mask over the input and keeps it sparse, while ExtremalMask (Enguehard, 2023) learns the perturbation as well as the mask and ContraLSP (Liu et al., 2024d) perturbs contrastively so that the result stays in distribution. TimeX (Queen et al., 2023) and TimeX++ (Liu et al., 2024c) instead train a separate model to imitate the forecaster and read the explanation from it. Shapley variants (Lundberg & Lee, 2017; Bento et al., 2021) and window-based methods (Tonekaboni et al., 2020; Leung et al., 2023) replace subsets of the input and divide the resulting change among them. Note that all of them solve an optimization problem for every input, which is what makes them expensive. P.2 LINES ADJACENT TO THIS WORK 

These three lines are adjacent to our setting rather than in it, so we place them here. 

Time series foundation models (TSFMs). A separate line of work pretrains one forecaster on a large corpus and applies it to unseen series without any further training (Goswami et al., 2024; Liu et al., 2025; Lee et al., 2026b). The widely used families are Chronos (Ansari et al., 2024), TimesFM (Das et al., 2024), Moirai (Woo et al., 2024), Timer (Liu et al., 2024b), TimeMoE (Shi et al., 2025), and TinyTimeMixer (Ekambaram et al., 2024). VisionTS (Chen et al., 2025) sits apart from all of them, since it forecasts with a masked autoencoder pretrained on images rather than on any time series. GIFT-Eval (Aksu et al., 2024) collects the benchmarks these models are compared on and keeps them apart from the pretraining corpus. None of this line reports what a released model reads for a given forecast step, and the libraries that ship these models place their inference path behind a no-gradient block, so the question has not been asked of them. 

Interpretable-by-design forecasters. N-BEATS (Oreshkin et al., 2020) decomposes a forecast into trend and seasonality stacks, the Temporal Fusion Transformer (Lim et al., 2021) exposes variable-selection weights and interpretable attention, and DLinear (Zeng et al., 2023) makes its H × L

weight matrix directly readable. The DLinear weight matrix is in fact horizon-resolved, but it is a property of one linear architecture rather than a general tool, and it describes the learned map instead of the evidence used on a given input. 

Frequency-based explanation. A smaller line of work explains along the frequency axis instead of the time axis, reporting which spectral bands a prediction relies on. SpectralX (Chung et al., 2024) perturbs features in the time-frequency plane and inverts the transform so that the original model can score the result, FLEXtime (Br¨ usch et al., 2025) optimizes a mask over the frequency bands of a filterbank, and FreqLens (Chen et al., 2026) learns the frequency bases from data for forecasting. 34 Preprint We leave all three out of Table 1, since a spectral explanation resolves neither the time axis nor the forecast step and therefore answers a different question from the one the table compares. Note that the horizon axis is independent of this choice, since a spectral explanation could be resolved per forecast step in the same way. 

Evaluation of explanations. Deletion curves (Samek et al., 2017) are the standard faithfulness probe, and several works document their failure modes, including perturbation artifacts (Brocki & Chung, 2022), retraining confounds (Hooker et al., 2019), and explanations that pass visual inspec-tion while being independent of the model (Adebayo et al., 2018). Every established metric assumes a model that outputs one label, so it scores a whole prediction and not one forecast step. 35 Preprint
