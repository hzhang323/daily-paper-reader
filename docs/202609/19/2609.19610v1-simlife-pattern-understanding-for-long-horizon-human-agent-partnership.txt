Abstract 

Understanding humans over long horizons requires agents to infer not only what people need in the moment, but also how routines form, why they repeat, and when they change. We introduce SIM LIFE , a scalable plat-form for simulating long-term household life with rich visual observations, ground-truth action logs, and synthetic dialogues with audio. Built on 

SIM LIFE , SIM LIFE -BP evaluates long-context pattern understanding: the ability to infer latent behavioral rules from weeks or months of everyday observations. The benchmark contains 106 episodes averaging 15.49 hours and 38.57 in-game days, and 1,439 question-answer pairs. Each task probes direct, counterfactual, noisy, and inverse reasoning under different levels of rule hints. Evaluating frontier models and architectures, we find that cur-rent models often achieve surface-level prediction without comprehensive rule understanding, rely on frequency-based heuristics rather than if-then reasoning over evidence, and struggle to adapt when behavioral patterns change. These findings suggest that long-context pattern understanding remains a major bottleneck for future embodied agents, while SIM LIFE 

opens a broader space for studying memory, personalization, adaptation, and long-horizon planning in everyday human-AI interaction 1.

1 Introduction 

Understanding humans’ implicit intents and motives has long been a fundamental challenge. In long-context settings, this challenge extends beyond recognizing what people need, feel, and believe in the moment (Ma et al., 2023; Peng et al., 2025) to discovering how they behave, why they repeat, and when those patterns change over time. Such understanding is essential for agents that must observe, reason, retain, and adapt over weeks or months of everyday life. Consider a household assistant observing that “Bob drinks coffee on weekdays, or after staying up late” . This rule cannot be inferred from any single event, nor from coffee occurrences alone: part of the evidence lies in seemingly unrelated prior-night activities that only become meaningful when linked to next-day behavior. Pattern understanding is therefore intrinsically long-context, requiring models to integrate dispersed evidence, rule out partial explanations, and apply the inferred rule to the current situation. To evaluate this form of long-context understanding under controlled yet realistic conditions, we need simulation environments that support extended timelines and rich everyday behaviors. Existing embodied simulation platforms provide valuable testbeds for embodied tasks (Puig et al., 2018; Ren et al., 2026; Zhou et al., 2026; Puig et al., 2024), but long-term household routines with diverse contextual cues remain difficult to simulate at scale. To address this gap, we propose SIM LIFE , a scalable platform for simulating long-term

Built on The Sims 4 2, SIM LIFE supports scripted simulations of multi-character household activities with rich visual observations, synthetic dialogues with audio, vivid animations, and diverse event categories (Figure 1). We use this platform as a controllable foundation for constructing long-context benchmarks grounded in everyday behavior. In this paper, we instantiate SIM LIFE as SIM LIFE -BP , a benchmark for behavioral pattern (BP) understanding: the ability to infer behavioral regularities, the rules behind them, and the temporal shifts that reshape them. SIM LIFE -BP comprises 106 episodes averaging 15.49 hours and 38.57 in-game days, with up to 48.23 hours and 120 days per episode, and 1,439 questions in total. Each episode simulates a latent behavioral pattern governed by one or more rules and is accompanied by ground-truth action logs and topic-centered synthetic dialogues with audio for contextual grounding. To evaluate pattern understanding systematically, SIM LIFE -BP combines two design axes: four question types that test direct, counterfactual, noisy, and inverse reasoning over inferred patterns, and three hint levels that vary how much rule information is provided, from explicit relevant conditions to abstract condition types to no prior hints. This design allows us to test whether models merely recognize surface regularities, or can robustly apply an inferred pattern across changing, distracting, and underspecified contexts. Our evaluation reveals three main findings. Models can achieve high question-level accuracy without complete rule understanding, as they often answer forward predictions correctly but fail to transfer the rule to reverse reasoning. Second, models often reduce pattern inference to simple heuristics, such as frequency counting, instead of performing the necessary if-then reasoning. Finally, models struggle to update their understanding when the underlying pattern changes. Together, these findings show that long-context pattern understanding remains far from solved. More broadly, beyond the benchmark, SIM LIFE opens up a wider space of long-horizon human-AI interaction challenges, providing a step toward embodied agents that can remember, adapt, and plan over extended periods of everyday life. 

2 Related Work 

Long Context Learning Long-context learning has long been studied across application domains, from early work on document summarization and long-document understand-ing (Luhn, 1958; Edmundson, 1969; Salton et al., 1994) to modern long-context understand-

2ing (Beltagy et al., 2020; Liu et al., 2024; Kamradt, 2023; Jelassi et al., 2024), dialogue and chat history modeling (Bei et al., 2026), agentic tool use (Yao et al., 2023; Acharya et al., 2025; Fu et al., 2024), multimodal understanding (Long et al., 2026; Chen et al., 2026; Yan et al., 2025a), and embodied AI with persistent memory (Wang et al., 2024; Dai et al., 2026). Architecturally, this progress has been supported by increasingly large context windows (Reid et al., 2024; Bai et al., 2025a; Peng et al., 2024; Team, 2025), test-time adaptation (Ma et al., 2026; Zhang et al., 2025), continual learning (Deng et al., 2025), and self-evolving agents (Gao et al., 2026). Everyday assistants naturally fall into this setting, yet the growing scale and complexity of long-context benchmarks raise a key question: what makes a task uniquely long-context, beyond simply requiring longer inputs? Echoing with Hsieh et al. (2024); Bai et al. (2024; 2025b), SIM LIFE -BP addresses this question by targeting intrinsically long-horizon rea-soning rather than local retrieval over chunked segments, requiring models to integrate and cross-reference evidence scattered across days, weeks, and modalities to infer latent behavioral patterns. 

Long Video Understanding Long video understanding benchmarks have expanded across duration and modality, covering hour-long QA (Wu et al., 2024), extreme-length compre-hension (Wang et al., 2025a), and egocentric scenarios (Grauman et al., 2022; Mangalam et al., 2023; Yang et al., 2025; Long et al., 2026; Chen et al., 2026; Yan et al., 2025a). Yet many still operationalize long-video understanding as retrieval or localization. SIM LIFE -BP in-stead targets intrinsically cross-temporal understanding, where answers require integrating implicit evidence across multiple days, modalities, and interactions. By combining continu-ous daily-life simulations with controlled scripts and sampled agent profiles, SIM LIFE -BP 

enables scalable, controllable evaluation of latent behavioral pattern inference. 

Pattern Recognition Pattern recognition spans perceptual regularity detection, sequential pattern mining, and abstract rule discovery (Bongard, 1970; Klahr & Dunbar, 1988; Josephson & Josephson, 1996; Agrawal & Srikant, 1995; Srikant & Agrawal, 1996; Mannila et al., 1997; Pei et al., 2001). Recent LLM benchmarks test rule induction from compact visual puzzles, symbolic examples, noisy observations, or programmatic input-output pairs (Chollet, 2019; Chollet et al., 2025; Hua et al., 2025; Li et al., 2025a;b). Long-context variants (Yan et al., 2025b; Kuratov et al., 2024) require aggregating many examples or scattered facts, but still use structured textual inputs. In contrast, SIM LIFE -BP evaluates behavioral pattern recognition from unstructured ultra-long multimodal observations, requiring models to infer reusable latent rules from sparse, perceptually grounded, temporally distributed evidence. 

3 SIM LIFE Benchmark 

3.1 Platform & Dataset SIM LIFE is built on The Sims 4 , where we simulate multi-character household activities observed from the egocentric perspective of a serving robot called Servo Bot . Each simulated day spans 6 AM to 10 PM in-game, corresponding to approximately 24 minutes in real time. Figure 2 illustrates one example simulated day. Characters (or Sims) are initialized with a behavioral profile specifying probabilistic activity preferences, which shape their daily routines. We then sample 466 in-game days as dataset building blocks. Each recorded simulated day is further enriched with synchronized ground-truth game logs filtered to Servo Bot’s field of view, spatialized conversational audio using Sim-specific voices, and context-generated dialogues. These dialogues range from natural everyday conversations to utterances that reference off-screen events or evolving situations.

Task Design We evaluate models using grouped question answering in a streaming setting. After exposing the model to a sufficiently long video history, we pause the video and ask four binary multiple-choice questions that probe complementary forms of pattern understanding: 1. Direct Prediction: Predict the most likely behavior given the current conditions. 2. Counterfactual Prediction: Predict the most likely behavior under a hypothetical change in conditions. 3. Noisy Counterfactual Prediction: Predict the most likely behavior under hypothetical conditions that include irrelevant information. 4. Inverse Counterfactual Reasoning: Infer the most plausible condition or cause given an observed behavior. Each behavioral pattern yields multiple tasks, inserted at query points chosen to cover different combinations of the pattern conditions. In the coffee example, we query the model on days corresponding to different combinations of weekday/weekend and with/without stayed-up-late. This allows us to test whether the model has learned the complete rule rather than a partial heuristic. In addition, we evaluate each task under three hint levels that vary how much rule infor-mation is provided. In Full Hints , the relevant conditions are directly specified, e.g., “the activity is related to whether it is a weekday” . In Partial Hints , only the abstract type of each condition is revealed, e.g., “the activity is related to the day of the week” ; this yields a closed hypothesis space while still requiring models to eliminate inconsistent rules from evidence. In No Hints , the model must infer the pattern entirely from observation (see Appendix D for more details). 4Finally, to avoid asking questions before sufficient evidence is available, we use the Partial Hints setting as a reference point for query timing. Specifically, we run a hypothesis-elimination procedure over the observations and insert tasks only after the remaining hypotheses make predictions consistent with the ground-truth rule at the selected query point. This ensures that each task is, in principle, answerable from the accumulated evidence. More details are provided in Appendix E. 

Benchmark Composition As is shown in Figure 1, SIM LIFE -BP contains 86 episodes for 

pattern understanding and 20 episodes for dynamic understanding . Pattern understanding episodes involve a single behavioral pattern, while dynamic understanding episodes involve multiple behavioral patterns and require models to identify distinct patterns as well as detect pattern shifts over time. Among them, 29 are solvable from vision alone, while 77 additionally require dialogue (audio). To further probe contextual reasoning, 30% of episodes follow counterintuitive patterns , which associate activities with unusual or implausible conditions that cannot be inferred through common sense alone. For example, the pattern “drink coffee after playing the piano” is unlikely to arise from prior knowledge. Models must therefore rely primarily on evidence from the video context rather than world priors when answering questions. 

4 Experiments 

We evaluate AI models on pattern understanding in ultra-long contexts. Our study addresses three core research questions: • RQ1: Can AI models infer patterns from ultra-long-term observations? We assess the overall capability of models to recognize and reason about behavioral patterns accumu-lated over extended observation periods. • RQ2: How do AI models develop pattern understanding over time? We probe the mod-els to explore how they incrementally build, refine, and revise their internal hypotheses across successive observations. • RQ3: How do AI models adapt to evolving patterns? We examine whether models can detect pattern changes, adapt their understanding accordingly, and retain knowledge of prior patterns without forgetting. 

4.1 Experimental Setup 

We design a series of experiments to investigate specific aspects of pattern understanding. 

4.1.1 Pattern Understanding Overall (RQ1) 

We designed experiments varying both the input modality and the degree of provided context. We evaluate models under four input conditions: • Vision + Language (V+L): Video frames (without audio) and dialogue transcripts. • Vision + Audio (V+A): Raw audio recordings of dialogue instead of transcript as part of the input. • Language Only (L): Event logs (action & dialogue) concatenated into a single input. This bypasses the vision process and uses ground-truth activities instead. • Language Only, noiseless (L-N): A filtered logs with main and directly related events. 

Model Choice We evaluate a broad set of models, including the Qwen3-VL family (4B, 8B, and 32B) (Bai et al., 2025a) with Chain-of-Thought (CoT) prompting; reasoning models with hybrid attention such as the Qwen3.5 (Qwen Team, 2026) and Gemma4 (Google, 2026) families, covering both dense and mixture-of-experts (MoE) variants; Qwen3-Omni (Xu et al., 2025) with an audio channel, and the Gemini (Google, 2025; Comanici et al., 2025) family as representative proprietary models. For vision-based input conditions, we follow the Socratic Models framework (Zeng et al., 2023): we divide each day’s video into four 5clips of approximately six minutes each, generate a summary for each clip, and concatenate all summaries with the task questions. For language-only variants, we concatenate the logs and questions into a single input pass. Implementation details are provided in Appendices J and K. We also include representative agentic baselines designed for temporal search, i.e. VideoTree (Wang et al., 2025b), and memory management, i.e. M3-Agent (Long et al., 2026). 

Hint Levels We evaluate models with three hint levels as introduced in Section 3.2. 

4.1.2 Trajectory Probing (RQ2) 

We evaluate four representative models (Gemini-2.5-flash, Gemini-3-flash-preview, Gemma-4-26B-A4B-it, and Qwen3.5-35B-A3B) under the Language Only , Partial Hints setting. For each day d in an episode, the model predicts whether the target activity will occur, yielding a daily belief trajectory Pd(yes ).Rather than evaluating only the final prediction, this day-by-day trajectory captures how a model’s belief shifts from uncertainty to confidence as evidence accumulates. Since a model’s internal decision process is not directly observable, we compare its trajectory against interpretable reference baselines as behavioral probes: if a model’s predictions closely track a baseline, this suggests that it may be following a similar decision strategy. We consider five reference baselines. Two are shallow heuristics that do not perform rule inference: (B1) frequency-based , predicting from the historical base rate, and (B2) last-day-based , extrapolating from the previous day’s outcome. Three are advanced references: (B3) 

ground-truth oracle, (B4) human predictions (Appendix H), and (B5) hypothesis-elimination ,which prunes candidate rules against accumulated evidence (Appendix E). 

4.1.3 Pattern Adaptation (RQ3) 

Patterns may evolve over time for arbitrary reasons. To evaluate models’ ability to adapt to evolving patterns, we design two experimental conditions. First, we examine whether models can rapidly adapt to pattern changes when the change is implicitly signaled through dialogue, such as when a character mentions that summer vacation has started, or the schedule gets tighter. This setting is part of our benchmark design, as described in Section 3.2. Second, after a model observes a sequence governed first by Pattern 1 and then by Pattern 2, we query it about events from the Pattern 1 period to assess whether prior knowledge is retained or overwritten. 

4.2 Evaluation Metrics 

For RQ1 and RQ3 , we report two primary metrics: All-Correct Rate (AC ), the proportion of tasks for which the model answers all sub-questions correctly, reflecting the consistency and completeness of its pattern understanding; Average Accuracy Rate (Acc ), the mean accuracy across all sub-questions within a task, as well as Sub-questions Accuracy (Sub Acc ), providing a finer-grained performance measure. For RQ2 , we analyze the models’ daily predicted probabilities for yes/no answers. We use the Brier score (Brier, 1950) to measure the discrepancy between predicted probabilities and ground-truth outcomes. Accuracy ( Acc ), balanced accuracy ( B-Acc ), positive F1-score ( F1 +), and averaged P(yes ) ( ¯ pyes ) are also reported for reference. 5.2 Trajectory Probing (RQ2) 

Table 2 reports the pairwise Spearman correlations among the Pd(yes ) trajectories across all sources, measuring how similarly each trajectory evolves over the chain. For Qwen, Gemma, and Gemini 2.5 Flash, the strongest non-self correlation is with the frequency-based baseline (28.8%, 32.2%, 38.5% respectively). These results suggest that, given partial hints, models tend to default to simple heuristics, like counting instances of the target activity, rather than evaluating candidate rules against the accumulated evidence. 

The exception is Gemini 3 Flash, whose closest non-self reference baseline is hypothesis elimination (40.5%), and is also the most aligned with human predictions (46.7%). By construction, the hypothesis-elimination baseline captures the sequential process of pruning candidate rules against observed evidence. Human predictions are strongly correlated with this baseline (78.1%), indicating that humans follow this rule-pruning process and do so effectively. These results suggest that Gemini 3 Flash exhibits a certain degree of hypothesis elimination and produces more human-like uncertainty trajectories over the chain. In contrast, the other models appear more strongly anchored by frequency and recency heuristics, showing weaker evidence of systematic hypothesis elimination. 

Representative Examples. Figure 4 unpacks the aggregate correlations on two chains selected from the human study (see Appendix H). The first case N0 ( “Mother paints every Tuesday” ) is the easiest pattern that hypothesis-elimination resolves within the first week (green area). Both Gemini 3 Flash and Gemma stay close to the human curve here, while Gemini 2.5 Flash picks up the signal early on but reverts to “no” for the end of the chain. 8The second case, N7 ( “Daughter plays games every Mon/Tue/Wed/Thu, but then plays only on weekends after she gets busy” ), contains a pattern shift at day 14. Humans and Gemini 3 Flash adapt quickly: Brier scores on post-pivot weekdays initially spike due to the old pattern, but they return to near zero within a week. In contrast, Gemini 2.5 Flash, Gemma, and Qwen remain locked onto the original rule and continue predicting “yes” on weekdays for the rest of the chain. 3

Three observations explain the alignment between aggregate ranking and the per-case shapes. First, Gemma and Qwen are heavily overconfident, placing 97–100% of predic-tions at P(yes )≤0.05 or ≥0.95, compared to 37% for humans, so each mistake incurs the full Brier penalty rather than the smaller cost of a hedged prediction. Second, on static conditional chains (N1–N3 in Appendix I), no model consistently executes the required if-then reasoning, instead substituting cheaper heuristics, such as keyword matching, pro-ducing trajectories more like the frequency-based reference baseline rather than hypothesis elimination. Third, on dynamic chains, regime-change dialogue appears in all post-pivot contexts but is referenced in only 1–12% of the post-pivot reasoning traces. Correspondingly, post-pivot Brier rises by +0.09 to +0.14 across the open models, showing they continue using the first pattern’s rule rather than resetting the hypothesis space. Overall, Gemini 3 Flash is the one model whose reasoning traces consistently show explicit cycle detection and post-pivot dialogue use, matching its stronger correlation with both hypothesis-elimination baseline and human prediction.

Hist Retrieval Shortcut. Notably, we expected Hist to be the hardest setting with the worst performance, because it requires retaining the earlier pattern while resisting interference from the current one; yet models generally perform better on Hist than P2 . We attribute this to a shortcut our design intended to avoid but that persists in full-context models: because pre-switch evidence remains verbatim in the context, models can answer Hist through local pattern-matching rather than by reasoning about the pattern transition. 96 Conclusion & Future Work 

We presented SIM LIFE , a platform for simulating long-term everyday life, and SIM LIFE -BP ,a benchmark for evaluating pattern understanding from ultra-long multimodal observations. Our results show that current models often rely on surface heuristics, fail to apply inferred rules robustly, and struggle to adapt to pattern shifts. These findings highlight long-context pattern understanding as an open challenge for embodied AI, and SIM LIFE provides a foundation for studying memory, personalization, adaptation, and long-horizon planning in everyday human-AI interaction.

17 A Limitations 

While SIM LIFE and SIM LIFE -BP have comprehensively revealed models’ pattern under-standing ability, several limitations remain. First, the behavioral patterns in SIM LIFE -BP 

are deterministic abstractions of real human behavior. Real-world habits are often stochastic, multi-factorial, and shaped by latent causes that may not be directly observable. We adopt deterministic rules not to claim that human behavior is rule-based, but to provide a con-trolled and verifiable setting in which pattern understanding can be evaluated with reliable ground truth. We view this as an intermediate step toward more realistic long-term human modeling, where the same core abilities: discovering regularities, ruling out inconsistent explanations, and adapting to change remain necessary. At the same time, although SIM LIFE provides rich multimodal observations and controllable long-term behavior, simulated household routines cannot fully capture the variability, ambiguity, and social complexity of real human lives. An important next step is to study whether models trained or evaluated on SIM LIFE -BP transfer to real-world long-term observation datasets, and whether simulated data can serve as useful supervision for building more adaptive assistants. Additionally, our human study is not a perfectly symmetric comparison between humans and models. To reduce cognitive load, human participants are shown a calendar-like interface and simplified structured summaries rather than raw ultra-long logs or videos (see Appendix H for more details). In dynamic-pattern settings, humans are also explicitly informed when a pattern may change, whereas models receive the corresponding signal only through the observation stream. These design choices make the human study more interpretable and feasible, but they also mean that human-model comparisons should be read as diagnostic rather than strictly controlled head-to-head evaluations. 

B Ethical Considerations 

Human-study Ethics 

An institutional review board determined that this study was exempt from ongoing re-view. We recruited 12 adult English-reading participants through personal outreach, and compensated each participant $20 for a study expected to take approximately one hour, following institutional participant-compensation guidelines. Participants were shown a consent statement describing the study purpose, duration, task format, recorded data, and research use. Only task responses, including symbolic inputs, slider responses, and text-based answers, were recorded. We did not store personally identifiable information, collect detailed demographic or geographic attributes, or collect protected attributes such as political views, health status, religious affiliation, or other sensitive personal information. Human-study results are reported only in aggregate, and individual response files are not publicly released. 

Risks and Intended Use 

The study uses simulated household activity schedules rather than real personal behavior traces, and participants were not asked to disclose personal experiences, private routines, demographic attributes, or sensitive information. We therefore consider the study to pose minimal risk to participants. 

Dataset Collection 

Our dataset and benchmark are constructed from recorded gameplay in The Sims 4 , used as a controllable simulation environment for non-commercial academic research. Data collection is controlled through custom scripts and community-developed modifications, with recordings captured from the perspective of an in-game robot agent and organized into video observations, event sequences, behavioral annotations, and learning targets. The original game narrative, artistic presentation, character design, and gameplay experience are 18 not the object of study, and the trained models are not designed to reproduce game assets or reconstruct the original gameplay environment. Data collection is limited to simulated daily activity; the Discover University downloadable content and community-developed modifications are used only to support and control in-game robot-agent behavior. 

C Controlled Generation of Behavioral Phenomena 

SIM LIFE inverts the usual record-then-annotate pipeline into a controlled generation pro-cedure: we specify a latent behavioral rule first, then assemble a long video that provably exhibits it by selecting and linking recordings from a finite pool of reusable pre-recorded days. This allows efficient benchmark construction with minimal annotation and filtering effort, and guarantees some extent of reproducibility. 4

The abstractions below build around one central object, the Phenomenon : a complete specification of a behavioral rule, and the blueprint every generated episode follows. Below it, an action, a condition, and a profile are the successive units that compose a phenomenon out of smaller parts. Above it, a schedule and a video chain are the successive steps that turn a phenomenon into an actual sequence of recordings. We walk through these six layers in that order, then trace one complete example end to end in C.7.

19 C.2 Conditions 

A condition turns an abstract behavioral rule into per-day ground truth: it determines on which days a target activity should or should not occur, and the resulting per-day labels are exactly what the benchmark later queries. Each condition targets an activity , an action performed within a time window by a specified set of participants, which we denote A

and formalize fully, as an Activity Specification , once it is needed for matching against recordings (C.5). Given a calendar of N days, a condition produces a binary label vector 

l ∈ { 0, 1 }N , where ld = 1 indicates that the activity is required on day d, and ld = 0 means the activity is required not to happen on day d.There are two families of conditions, corresponding to the two ways a rule can be driven: 

temporal conditions are driven by calendar structure (e.g., the day of the week), and activity-based conditions are driven by another activity (e.g., an event the day before). Because real behavioral patterns are rarely a single rule but a conjunction, exclusion, or alternation of simpler ones, conditions targeting the same activity can be merged; each condition therefore carries an operation field governing how it combines with others (C.2.3).

C.5 Schedule Generation 

A schedule compiles a declarative phenomenon into a concrete, date-by-date requirement: it turns “this rule holds” into “here is what day 12 must contain.” The same phenomenon compiled from a different start date, or with different independently-sampled trigger days, yields a different schedule. This makes it possible to generate multiple valid schedules with one phenomenon, as well as doing rejection sampling until getting a valid schedule. Each requirement in a schedule is expressed as an Activity Specification , which filters the pool of recorded days and returns those that contain a matching activity.

Each SIM LIFE -BP task poses four types of multiple-choice questions over the same long video: direct prediction , counterfactual prediction , noisy counterfactual prediction , and inverse counterfactual reasoning . All four are built on a single abstraction, the Day Condition Set 

(Appendix C.6), and together probe whether a model has recovered the rule governing a character’s behavior. The design factors into a shared foundation and three orthogonal axes. The foundation is the Day Condition Set (Appendix D.1): the truth table of the behavioral rule a task targets. The three axes are what is asked (the four question types, Appendix D.2), how much of the rule is revealed (hint levels, Appendix D.3), and which behavioral phase is targeted (standard vs. history questions, Appendix D.4). Appendix D.5 composes the three axes into one rendered question, and Appendices D.6 and D.7 document two implementation details: noise-condition selection and the natural-language rendering pass. 

D.1 Setup: the Day Condition Set

D.6 Selecting a Noise Condition 

A noise counterfactual is only meaningful if the introduced condition is genuinely uninfor-mative about the main activity. We do not pick such a condition by hand or by semantic intuition; instead we require it to be empirically independent of the main activity A within the specific video chain, and we verify this with a 2 × 2 contingency table. 

Four-cell independence test. First, we use the occurrence of an action as the noise condi-tion. When the ground truth contains an activity condition, we reuse the first one’s slot parameters (participant, time zones, action-hierarchy level, and condition type) and search its action pool, excluding the true cause itself. When the ground truth is purely temporal, we synthesize a fixed condition right_after , the same participant as the main activity, a full observable-day window (06:00–22:00), and subcategory level. Socially entangled action categories are excluded from the pool, since their occurrence is too context-dependent to serve as clean noise. The search is exhaustive over the pool and returns the first X that passes the test; if none does, no noise counterfactual is generated for that task. 

D.7 Generation and Rendering 

Questions are produced in two stages. First, a templating stage emits each question with structured metadata: the differing conditions for a counterfactual ( if_clause ), the irrelevant action for a noise counterfactual ( noise_framing ), and the negated outcome plus candidate condition lists for an inverse counterfactual ( premise , icf_body , icf_body_opposite ). Sec-ond, an LLM rendering pass converts these templates into fluent, mood-correct natural language: indicative for direct questions and subjunctive for the three counterfactual types, preserving all logical content and the exact character names and clock-time spans. 

E Hypothesis Elimination For Task Insertion 

A benchmark task is inserted at a given point only if every plausible reading of the hint that is still consistent with what has been observed so far agrees on the answer. This section formalizes that criterion: we enumerate every hypothesis a hint permits, discard those contradicted by the recorded days, and query the model only once the survivors all agree. We use the partial-hint setting as the reference, since observations, hint, and question together guarantee this criterion is satisfiable under both the partial-hint and full-hint settings. In the no-hint setting, sufficiency cannot be formally defined because the hypothesis space is unbounded; we use it instead to evaluate open-ended pattern reasoning under conditions closer to the real world. We ground each definition below in one running example: a partial hint stating that a target activity “painting” is related to the day of the week, with the true rule (unknown to the reasoner) being “happens on Mondays and Fridays.”

The Sims 4 recordings used in SimLife contain silent “simlish” mouth animations rather than audible speech. We therefore synthesize English dialogue after recording. The dialogue pipeline has two purposes: • Original dialogue generation : makes each chat interaction sound natural and temporally aligned with the video. • Prompted dialogue adaptation : modifies generated dialogue to introduce phenomenon-relevant topics, mention activities that are not directly visible, or indicate the change-point event on the pivot day of a dynamic episode. 

F.1 Original Dialogue Generation Chatting session detection. Original dialogue generation starts from the trimmed game-play video and game action log. The action log is used to identify chatting sessions, defined as contiguous intervals in which two or more Sims are plausibly engaged in the same log-recorded chat event. Failed, canceled, idle, and menu-level actions are removed, and overlapping chat actions among nearby Sims are grouped into sessions. Sessions are merged only when participants remain temporally and spatially close, and split when a participant leaves or begins a separate chat with someone outside the session. 

Visual speech refinement. Because log-derived action boundaries are often coarser than visible speech, we refine each session using mouth-motion cues from the video. Faces are detected in sampled frames, assigned to characters using reference images, and analyzed with lip landmarks. We compute a mouth aspect ratio, MAR = 

> 13

∑(i,j)∈P∥xi − xj∥∥xright − xleft ∥ , (10) where P contains inner-lip landmark pairs and the denominator is the mouth-corner distance. Frames with sufficiently large mouth opening are treated as visual speech, while wide-smile cases are suppressed using mouth-width geometry. The visual speech intervals are then fused with the action-log session boundaries. 

Speaker timeline construction. For each fused session, we construct a speaker timeline: an ordered sequence of speaker slots with start time, end time, speaker identity, and word budget. Visual speech intervals receive highest priority, while action-log rows provide softer evidence for who is likely speaking. Remaining gaps are filled by rotating among active participants, and very short slots are constrained to brief interjections. 

Dialogue generation. We generate dialogue in two model-assisted steps using Qwen3.5-35B-A3B. First, the model captions the session clip, describing visible participants, facial expressions, mouth motion, interaction context, environment, and mood from Servo Bot’s first-person viewpoint. Second, the model is prompted with the caption, speaker timeline, activity context, and character profiles to generate exactly one utterance per slot. 

Speech synthesis and audio mixing. Each utterance is synthesized using Qwen3-TTS-12Hz-1.7B-CustomVoice 6 with character-specific voice assignments and mixed into the video. 33 F.2 Prompted Dialogue Adaptation 

After producing the original dialogue track, we adapt selected sessions to support phe-nomena whose target rules cannot be fully inferred from visible frames alone. Dialogue adaptation is controlled through additional prompt instructions in the dialogue generation stage. 

Invisible activity completion. Some phenomenon-relevant activities are not directly visi-ble because they occur outside the camera view or during occlusion. In these cases, dialogue can make the missing activity inferable by referring to activities that have already happened, are currently being planned, or are about to happen. Such references must be consistent with the underlying action log and schedule, and are restricted to same-day activities to avoid revealing underlying patterns. 

Dynamic Episodes. For dynamic episodes , the household follows one pattern before a pivot day and another pattern after it. The pivot day contains a change-point event that explains or motivates this shift. During prompted dialogue adaptation, we inject this event into one of the pivot-day conversations without explicitly stating the full before–after rule. For example, a pivot-day dialogue session involving Mother Sim may mention that she is preparing for an art market, causing her to switch from painting every other day to painting every day. 

F.3 Constraints on Adapted Dialogue 

Prompted adaptation must remain temporally aligned with the original speaker timeline, visually and causally consistent with the recorded video, action log, and character profiles, and must not directly reveal the benchmark answer. The dialogue may provide clues, reminders, or references to relevant events, but the target phenomenon must still be inferred from the full multimodal context. 

F.4 Quality Control 

We apply quality control at both the model-generation stage and the human-inspection stage. During original dialogue generation, the session caption is first generated from the video clip. The dialogue-generation model then receives this caption together with the speaker timeline, activity context, and game log information. Before generating the final utterances, the model is prompted to check whether the caption is consistent with the log-derived session context, including visible participants, activities, and temporal ordering. After dialogue generation, we further inspect generated sessions using our visualization platform. We conduct manual spot checks to verify that the dialogue is temporally aligned, visually plausible, and consistent with the underlying activity logs. This inspection is used to identify obvious captioning errors, speaker mismatches, timing issues, or dialogue that reveals more information than intended.

H.1 User Interface

A compact history view in the form of a calendar ( 4) summarizes whether the target activity occurred on previous days. J.4 Multi-Day Chain Handling 

Each evaluation task specifies an ordered chain of video recordings spanning one or more simulated days, along with a stop_day_position and a global stop time in seconds marking the task’s temporal scope. The pipeline assembles the diary text as follows: for days before the stop day, all four cached [Window summary] blocks are included. For the stop day, windows whose entire interval precedes the stop time are included in full; the window that straddles the stop time is re-summarized using only frames up to the stop time; and subsequent windows are excluded. This ensures the model is never exposed to events beyond the question’s designated horizon. 

J.5 Agentic Baselines Adapting M3-Agent to S IM LIFE -BP. M3-Agent (Long et al., 2026) handles long videos by separating memory construction from question answering. Instead of reasoning over the full video directly, it first converts the video into an explicit memory graph. To build this graph, M3-Agent splits the video into short clips, detects faces and speakers with existing tools, transcribes speech, and uses a fine-tuned Qwen2.5-Omni-7B model to generate structured descriptions for each clip. These descriptions use entity placeholders for people and voices, which are linked across clips to form a graph of multimodal memories. At test time, a fine-tuned Qwen3-32B model serves as the reasoning module: it repeatedly retrieves relevant memories from the graph using natural-language queries and answers questions based on the retrieved information. In our SimLife adaptation, we do not train or fine-tune any M3-Agent models. Instead, we directly use the released M3-Agent models for inference and only modify the memory graph construction process. SimLife consists of reusable Sims video units, and each QA task combines multiple units into a longer chain. Processing every full chain from scratch would therefore be redundant. We first precompute clip-level memories for each reusable unit, including face clusters, speaker information, and Qwen2.5-Omni captions. For each QA task, we then assemble the memory graph by replaying the cached unit memories in the order specified by the task chain, mapping local entity placeholders to global identities, and constructing two graphs: one with audio information and one without. During evaluation, we select the appropriate graph according to whether audio is available in the task setting. We make three lightweight SimLife-specific changes to improve reliability. First, instead of using ASR-generated transcripts, we use the clean dialogue text provided by SimLife when constructing textual memories. The input remains the original MP4 video, so the multimodal model can still perceive visual content and ambient audio, while the memory 44 graph receives accurate transcript text. Second, we link the four main characters, Father Sim, Mother Sim, Daughter Sim, and Son Sim, to fixed global face identities using reference avatar embeddings. This improves identity consistency across reused video units. Third, we add the date and weekday from the chain calendar to each memory and store them as metadata, enabling more reliable retrieval for time-related questions. 

Adapting VideoTree to S IM LIFE -BP. VideoTree (Wang et al., 2025b) performs long video understanding by building a query-adaptive, hierarchical video representation to aid LLM reasoning in question answering. Rather than uniformly sampling frames, it performs adaptive breadth expansion to identify representative keyframes and then uses relevance-guided depth expansion to further explore the hierarchical structure to yield a tree-based representation of the video where the leaves are a question-focused subset of frames. In our SimLife adaptation, we precompute frame-level EVA-CLIP-8B features once per reusable video unit and cache them on disk. When constructing a task, we assemble the full feature tensor by concatenating cached unit features in the order specified by the task chain, rather than re-encoding the same video multiple times. Caption resolution is also lazy: instead of loading all captions upfront, we store a narration manifest per chain that records each unit’s caption path, global time offset, and calendar metadata (day index, day of week, and date). During tree construction and QA, only the captions for the sparse set of selected tree frames are loaded from disk. We make a few SimLife-specific changes to the prompts given to the LLM at the breadth-expansion stage and the final question answering stage. First, we inject day and wall-clock time annotations into the narration: each caption line is prefixed with its simulated time (mapped from video-local seconds to the 06:00–22:00 simulation day) and grouped under a day header showing the date and weekday. This provides the LLM with explicit temporal grounding for time-sensitive questions. For tasks in the audio-available setting, we augment the breadth-expansion prompt with dialogue, inserting speech events that fall within a five-second window of each sampled frame. This allows the model to incorporate conversational context when scoring frame relevance, without modifying the tree-construction algorithm itself. Finally, VideoTree’s original prompts are designed for five-option multiple-choice questions, so we use SIM LIFE -specific prompt templates that format questions with two options (A and B) while preserving the relevance-scoring mechanism needed for breadth expansion. 

K Text-Only Baseline Implementation Details 

We evaluate two families of text-only baselines that receive no video frames — only the structured activity logs extracted during post-processing. These baselines serve two distinct roles: Text-Only (Noisy) measures how much can be inferred from the raw, automatically extracted log stream, and Text-Only (Noiseless) provides an oracle upper bound by supply-ing only the task-relevant subset of that stream. Both families are evaluated matching the hint variants used in all other baselines to enable direct comparison. We use vllm 0.19.0 with 1~4 Nvidia A40 GPUs for inference task. It takes between several hours to 7 days to fully run the evaluation for one model.

45 Lines within a day are sorted first by in-game timestamp, then by original insertion order to break ties. Day sections are delimited by a header of the form === Day N (day_of_week, date) === , allowing the model to track the calendar structure of the simulation. 

Noisy baseline concatenation. For each task, per-day log files are concatenated in chrono-logical order up to and including the day designated by stop_day_position . Two log variants are used depending on the task type: an actions-only file (omitting dialogue) for standard tasks, and a file containing both actions and overlay-side dialogue for tasks that require audio–visual reasoning. For the final day in a chain, if the task specifies a mid-video stopping point ( stop_video_time_local ), the last day’s text is rebuilt from the underlying log file by filtering events whose video timestamp precedes the cut. When the resulting prompt exceeds the model’s input budget, the earliest days are dropped until the truncated log fits. The retained suffix is the most temporally proximate context, which is generally most relevant to questions about behavior up to stop_day_position. 

Noiseless baseline. The noiseless variant loads pre-materialized, oracle-filtered log files. Each file contains three sections — one per hint variant — and each section retains only: (i) day-boundary headers, (ii) the condition-side rows that the phenomenon predicate depends on, and (iii) occurrences of the target activity on fully observed prior days. 

K.2 Prompt Structure 

Both baselines use an identical two-turn prompt structure (system + user). The system message instructs the model to answer only from the provided logs, to attend to day boundaries and day-of-week patterns, and to emit a strictly formatted response: 

> Your entire response MUST consist of exactly two lines: Line 1: Thought: <at most 4 short sentences of reasoning> Line 2: Answer: <a single capital letter A, B, C, or D>

The noiseless system prompt additionally notes that the input is a condensed observation history produced by oracle filtering, and that information beyond the observation cutoff should not be assumed. The user message presents the concatenated log (or condensed history), followed by the question and lettered answer options. 

K.3 Retry Policy 

Each variant call allows up to three attempts. Attempts beyond the first append a tightening hint to the system message, which: (i) reminds the model that its previous attempt exhausted the token budget without emitting an answer, (ii) caps the Thought to three sentences, (iii) forbids nested counterfactual reasoning and day-by-day re-enumeration — the specific failure modes observed during development on reasoning-capable models. Retry delays are 0 s, 5 s, and 15 s for attempts 1, 2, and 3 respectively. The final attempt’s output is persisted regardless of success; an unparseable response is scored as incorrect.
