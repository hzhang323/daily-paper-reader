Abstract 

Time Series Anomaly Detection (TSAD) has received increasing attention, driven by the growing availability of complex time series data. This surge has led to the development of numerous detection methods, as well as a variety of benchmarks aimed at thoroughly evaluating their performance. However, most existing detectors remain largely agnostic to domain context, overlooking explainability and inter-pretability. One of the main reasons for this gap is that current benchmarks primarily focus on detection accuracy, and only few of them evaluate spatial explainability. Moreover, no benchmark currently provides sufficiently rich semantic annotations to support the generation of human-understandable interpretations of anomalies. To address these limitations, we introduce SHAD (Scality High-dimensional Anomaly 

Detection benchmark), a fully annotated benchmark composed of 215 multivariate, high-dimensional time series collected from real-world distributed cloud storage systems operated by Scality. The proposed dataset includes rich contextual in-formation, covering three families of anomalies with varying degrees of severity. As further contribution, we provide a foundation for future work by evaluating baseline methods for Detection , Explainability , and Interpretability , covering all stages of a TSAD pipeline. For Detection , we benchmark a wide range of existing anomaly detectors, testing their effectiveness on the proposed real-world dataset. Then, we consider explainability by evaluating whether measuring the contribution of each dimension in the generated anomaly score can provide accurate anomaly attributions. Finally, for interpretability , we investigate the effectiveness of frozen LLM baselines in localizing and interpreting anomalies, paving the way for future research towards interpretable and explainable TSAD.

Density-based methods instead detect anomalous behaviors by estimating how isolated observations or subsequences are in a representation space [7, 14, 53, 27]. Finally, prediction-based methods ( fore-casting [95] or reconstruction-based [80]) estimate expected temporal behavior and use prediction error as the anomaly signal. Despite the large availability of anomaly detectors, most of them remain agnostic, therefore, achieving interpretability on a given domain is not straightforward. 

Interpretable and Explainable Anomaly Detection. The concerns about the black-box nature of deep-learning based approaches have led to efforts to make model behaviours more transparent and human-understandable [35]. Such studies usually refer to the notions of interpretability and 

explainability . However, these two terms are often used interchangeably, without a clear distinction. Thus, we now define these concepts as they are used throughout this paper, by providing informal definitions (illustrated in Figure 2(a,b)), as they inherently relate to human understanding. 

Explainability: We define explainability as the ability of a model to provide spatially grounded attributions that highlight which components of the input contribute to the detected anomaly. 

Explainable time series anomaly detection methods are broadly divided into intrinsically explainable models and model-agnostic approaches [38]. The former build human-understandable representa-tions (e.g., decision trees ) directly from data [59], while the latter decouple explanations from the underlying model [70]. In TSAD, forecasting and reconstruction methods [95, 80] are inherently explainable. Among model-agnostic techniques, some rely on input perturbations to observe changes in model outputs [48]. LIME [70] explains individual predictions via a local surrogate model learned through weighted linear regression, while Anchors [71] extends this idea using logical rules. Other methods assign feature importance scores [13, 82]. Among these, SHAP [57] is based on Shapley values [92], and has been extended to time series [62]. In [51] the authors propose DuoGAT, a TSAD approach based on a directed and weighted graph structure, and provide an explainability approach by hierarchically tracking critical time points to provide time-oriented reasoning. Finally, global explanation approaches approximate models with explainable ones [26, 23] or extract decision rules [44, 48, 21]. 

Interpretability: We define interpretability as the ability to produce a human-understandable interpretation of the nature and context of anomalies. 

Interpretable approaches remain relatively limited. MacroBase [12] helps users focus on relevant portions of high-volume data streams, providing interpretable insights based on key attributes recur-rence. In the biomedical domain, Boubekki et al. [18] propose a method for interpreting models for ECG analysis through time-localized clustering of internal representations. Their approach associates temporally localized patterns with clinically meaningful conditions, supporting the interpretation of what the detected abnormal patterns represent. Advances in large language models (LLMs) offer a promising direction, enabling context-rich interpretations by leveraging model outputs and auxiliary information [40]. While many works use LLMs for anomaly detection itself [39, 88, 69], others explore their role in human-understandable data analysis [97, 63]. LLMs have also been applied to integrate time series with external textual context, improving prediction and decision-making [45, 47], while agent-based and tool-augmented frameworks enhance reasoning and root cause analysis [90, 108]. Among existing proposals [33, 107], only a few support multimodal data [109, 96]. Among these, ChatTS [96] is a recent multimodal LLM capable of reasoning over 3multivariate time series and enables natural language interaction to interpret patterns, relationships, and anomalies.

Limitations of Existing Benchmarks. 

Despite the available benchmarks, there are several limitations that need to be addressed: 

L1 (Lack of end-to-end benchmarks ): existing benchmarks mainly focus on anomaly detection itself, thoroughly evaluating the detectors’ accuracy but overlooking explainability and interpretability ,which are critical parts of a TSAD pipeline. 

L2 (Lack of fine-grained anomaly labels ): existing benchmarks only provide binary labels indicating whether an anomaly is present at a given time step, without specifying which dimension or sensor is affected, nor characterizing the nature of the anomaly itself. 

L3 (Lack of contextual knowledge ): existing benchmarks do not include rich semantic annotations and contextual information, hindering the generation of human-readable anomaly interpretations. 

Objectives and Research Questions. In response to these gaps we propose a real-world, high-dimensional curated dataset with rich anomaly descriptions and and fine-grained documentation. We also include an experimental evaluation designed to answer the following research questions: 

RQ1 (Detection ): How do current state-of-the-art time series anomaly detection methods perform on a real-world, high-dimensional benchmark, and how do they compare in terms of effectiveness? 

RQ2 (Explanation ): Can TSAD methods achieve explainability through dimension-level attribution? 

RQ3 (Interpretation ): Can frozen LLM models provide accurate anomaly interpretations?

Experiment Design. Our experiment framework runs one specific baseline workload consistent across all experiments. The workload runs uninterrupted for 18 hours with a one-minute telemetry ingestion interval, yielding 1000–1050 data points per experiment. The workload generates GET-heavy (i.e., dominated by object retrieval requests) mixed S3 traffic via the warp benchmarking tool, using 16 concurrent workers, 15k initial objects at experiment start, 100KiB max randomized object size, and an S3 operation distribution of approximately 95% GET , 4% STAT , 0.8% PUT , and 0.4% 

DELETE by weight. All experiments are conducted on top of this baseline workload, which simulates a representative workload of a typical storage cluster serving read-heavy object retrieval. The experimental framework enables the controlled simulation of anomalies that affect otherwise nominal system traffic. All anomalous events are defined by their trigger time, their total duration, their targeted components, and extra failure-specific parameters. SHAD includes 3 general anomalies: 

server failures , disk failures , and storage node failures (see Figure 3). 

(i) Disk failures are triggered by instantly severing a server’s connection with the targeted data disk(s). 

Single disk failures target one disk on a specific server. Simultaneous failures target multiple disks at once, while Asynchronous failures target multiple disks at different timestamps. For the latter, we further distinguish three sub-patterns: cascading failures occur in close succession with substantial temporal overlap; rolling failures are spaced further apart with partial overlap; independent failures are fully separated with no overlap. Disk failures are fixed by rebinding the target’s device driver to its server. The impact of disk failures can vary from minimal to high depending on the number of disks targeted; despite the compact topology, RING’s distributed architecture is resilient to disk anomalies by design. Therefore, multi-disk failure scenarios produce stronger signals. 

(ii) Server failures are triggered by tampering with the target server’s network interface. Single server failures drop all inbound traffic from the rest of the cluster, and are bound to a certain duration. This simulates a complete network partition, as would occur during a switch failure or a severe network misconfiguration. Asynchronous failures target multiple servers at different timestamps, and, like disk failures, are categorized in cascading, rolling, independent . Degradation failures progressively increase latency and packet loss between the target server and the rest of the cluster, following either a linear ramp or staged discrete steps. This simulates progressive network deterioration, such as a slowly failing network interface or increasing link congestion. Server failures are fixed by reversing network changes and returning the server to its nominal state. Server failures have a strong impact on the rest of the cluster and thus heavily influence metrics across the entire system. Notice that 

Simultaneous server failures are excluded as they would essentially render the cluster non-functional. 

(iii) Storage node failures are triggered by instantly killing the process(es) corresponding to a configurable number of software nodes on the targeted storage server. Dataset Characteristics. SHAD contains 215 multi-variate time series, whose composition is detailed in Figure 3. Each series contains ∼1021 data points, which were sampled with a 1-minute resolution across 171 features (listed in Appendix B). The anomalies are annotated by Scality experts, who su-pervised each planned experiment, ensuring that the injected failure type, timing, and affected components were accurately recorded and consistently reflected in the released metadata. Anomalies are labeled on two layers: Top-level labeling assigns one binary value per time point, corresponding to whether an anomaly is ongoing and aligning with the format of existing benchmarks [54]. Dimension-level labeling similarly flags anomalies per time point, but only to the dimensions corresponding to the root cause com-ponents they directly target. In addition, to support interpretability and explainability studies, each time series in SHAD is accompanied by a structured XML annotation (see Figure 5), with both global and fine-grained information. For each series, we provide whether an anomaly is present, its criticality, and its category, along with a textual explanation. Then, fine-grained per-dimension annotations include a textual explanation and, in case of anomalies, failure-specific parameters, also incorporating the set of other dimensions involved by the same anomaly, allowing for linking related anomalous behaviors and reconstructing the full system-level event. To our knowledge, no existing TSAD benchmark provides comparable semantic annotations, while SHAD enables new forms of evaluation for both explainability and interpretability. 6LLMs

4 Experimental Evaluation on SHAD 

We now discuss the experiments aimed at addressing our research questions. For RQ1 (Detection ), we assess the effectiveness of 18 state-of-the-art detection methods covering a wide range of Time Series Anomaly Detection processes (see Table 17 in Appendix E for the full list) on SHAD. Both Semi-Supervised (trained on nominal data) and Unsupervised approaches are included, using the optimized hyperparameters from the TSB-AD benchmark [54]. Performance is evaluated using VUS-PR (a robust, threshold-independent metric) with a 25-point buffer [66, 17]. For RQ2 (Explanation ): we evaluate the contribution of each dimension in the final anomaly score for the top performing method of each supervision category. We evaluate their performance using the NDCG metric [89], since this metric rewards well ranked features diagnosis. For RQ3 (Interpretation ), we evaluate the ability of frozen LLMs to interpret anomalies using a single call. Each time series is provided to the LLM under different representations: (i) Catch22 features [56] for each dimension; (ii) a multi-panel image with one subplot per dimension; and (iii) a natural language description of the time series image generated by the same LLM 2. The LLM is prompted with a structured questionnaire (Figure 6) to: (i) detect anomaly presence, (ii) identify at least one affected dimension, (iii) localize the anomaly in time, (iv) classify it, and (v) assess its severity. We consider F1-score for classification tasks (presence, type, severity) and accuracy for localization tasks, averaged over 5 runs. We compare five LLMs (listed in Figure 6), and we also performed an additional evaluation with models from the same family (i.e., Mistral) with different capacities to evaluate whether the LLM size has an impact on interpretability. Finally, LLM experiments are repeated under two supervision levels to simulate an end-to-end TSAD pipeline that covers all three stages ( detection, explanation, interpretation ). In particular, we simulate the availability of an always correct anomaly detector (i.e., level 1, which provides the LLM with information about anomaly presence), and the availability of an always correct anomaly explainer (i.e., level 2, which provides the LLM with information about anomaly presence 

and the affected system components).

4.3 RQ3: Time Series Anomaly Interpretation 

Comparison with different representations. Figure 9(a) reports the performance of LLMs across different input representations for the interpretability task. Overall, results are very low, highlighting the difficulty of interpreting high-dimensional real-world time series. For anomaly presence ( 1

), most of the LLMs always predicted an anomaly regardless of the representation, suggesting that irregular patterns and high-dimensionality lead the models to interpret variability as anomalies. This led to very similar average results across all models (0.48, 0.47, 0.43 for Descriptions, Images, and Catch22, resp.). For anomaly type , performance remains poor, with the best F1-score from Images ( 21% ), followed by Catch22 ( 19% ) and descriptions ( 14% ), and a noticeable bias toward Disk Failures. The anomaly criticality task ( 3 ) is even more challenging, with a maximum of 

16% (Images), showing the inability to assess severity without contextual knowledge. Dimension localization ( 4 ) is among the hardest tasks, with near-zero accuracy ( 1% , 2% , 1% for Catch22, Images, and descriptions), reflecting the difficulty of identifying anomalous dimensions in large multivariate data. For time localization ( 5 ), results are slightly better but still low ( 7% , 7% ,

2% for Images, Catch22, and Descriptions). Overall, all the results highlighted the complexity of interpreting time series using LLMs without any type of supervision. While the results are generally low, providing Images was the best option for three tasks ( 2 , 3 , 4 ) and the second best option for the others, suggesting that visual representations provide a slightly clearer overview of the series. 

Comparison with different LLMs. Figure 9(a) compares the performance of 5 LLMs for each representation across the five tasks. For anomaly presence ( 1 ), almost all models behaved identically (≈ 47% across all representations), showing a strong bias towards anomalies. The only exception was Grok (especially with the Catch22 representation) which, in contrast, judged most series as normal. This is consistent with recent evidence that Grok exhibits a distinctive behavioral profile compared to other frontier LLMs [105]. For anomaly type and criticality ( 2 , 3 ) the best LLM is Claude (0.23 and 0.19 , resp.), while Grok was the best for dimension localization ( 4 ), but with only 4% of accuracy. Finally, Claude was the best model for time localization ( 5 ), with 12%. Overall, all the models showed very similar behaviours (we provide the full results in Appendix E), showing that interpreting anomalies is a significant challenge.

5 Conclusion and Future directions 

We proposed SHAD , a novel end-to-end benchmark composed of real-world, high-dimensional time series, that covers all the steps in TSAD pipelines ( detection, explanation , and interpretation ). SHAD includes both normal and anomalous series, with anomalies affecting different components and severity levels, and provides per-dimension labels along with rich textual annotations for each dimension and anomaly. Our experimental evaluation concluded that (i) SOTA anomaly detection approaches are accurate but not perfect for all anomalies in SHAD, (ii) Explanability on SHAD cannot be natively solved with existing TSAD methods, and (iii) frozen LLMs are not able to provide correct interpretations of anomalies. Finally, SHAD enables new research directions: in detection , studying robustness when directly affected metrics are removed; in explainability , rigorously assessing variable contributions via per-dimension labels and developing dedicated explainability methods trained on SHAD’s annotations, rather than relying on native detector attribution scores; and in interpretability ,investigating how reliable dimension-level explanations can be provided as structured, semantically enriched context to LLMs, enabling the generation of human-understandable anomaly interpretations grounded in both data and semantics. 10 Acknowledgments and Disclosure of Funding

18 A Related Work 

A.1 Existing benchmarks Existing Benchmarks: How do we evaluate time series anomaly detection? Research in TSAD has led to the introduction of numerous datasets (see Table 2), which have been considered in multiple surveys [67, 75, 83, 54], consistently highlighting the absence of a one-size-fits-all anomaly detector. Consequently, efforts have aimed to establish benchmarks, whose main characteristics are detailed in Table 1, incorporating multiple datasets from various domains for comprehensive evaluation. NAB [50] provides 58 labeled real-world and artificial time series, and primarily focuses on real-time anomaly detection for streaming data. It comprises diverse domains such as AWS server metrics, online advertisement clicking rates, real-time traffic data, and a collection of Twitter mentions of large publicly-traded companies. TODS [46] refines synthetic criterion and includes five anomaly scenarios categorized as point-global, pattern-contextual, pattern-shapelet, pattern-seasonal, and pattern-trend. TimeEval [75] comprises a collection of datasets (both real/synthetic and univariate/multivariate) with point and sequence anomalies. In addition, this benchmark has been filtered such that there is no time series that has a normal/abnormal ratio above 0.1, and that at least one method performs more than 0.8 AUC-ROC for each time series. TimeSeAD [87] analyzed widely used multivariate datasets and introduced a recall-consistent evaluation metric that accounts for temporal correlations, benchmarking 28 deep-learning based methods, while Experimental-TSAD [103] compared 17 state-of-the-art anomaly detection methods using both point-based and range-based metrics across various data sizes, anomaly rates, feature dimensions, and temporal patterns. TSB-UAD [67] is a comprehensive benchmark designed for evaluating univariate TSAD, including real-world and synthetic datasets. Exathlon [38] is proposed for explainable anomaly detection over high-dimensional time series data. It is constructed based on real data traces from an Apache Spark cluster, and was designed to address the pitfalls of previous benchmarks [94]. It features a single anomalies per series, which in most cases is artificially. Aiming at addressing the challenge of designing truly representative benchmarks, TSB-AD [54] introduced the first large-scale, heterogeneous, and meticulously curated dataset, also incorporating latest advances in foundation model-based methods, highlighting their potential for TSAD. Finally, TAB [68] is a recent and comprehensive benchmark that provides an extensive algorithmic comparison, including 19 statistical methods, 18 neural network–based approaches, and 7 foundation model–based methods.

A.3 Interpretability and Explainability Interpretable and Explainable Anomaly Detection. Explainable machine learning techniques can be broadly categorized into intrinsically explainable models and model-agnostic approaches [38]. The former category focuses on building a human-readable representation (e.g. decision trees ) from data [59], while the latter separate explanations from the ML model [70]. From the perspective of anomaly detection approaches, all forecasting/reconstruction methods [95, 80] are inherently explainable. Among model-agnostic approaches, several methods are based on perturbing the inputs and observing the response of the classifier [48]. LIME [70] explains individual predictions by learning a local surrogate model that approximates the original model in the neighborhood of a given instance, using a weighted linear regression formulation. This framework was extended by Anchors [71], which replaces the linear model with logical rules. Other approaches assign a numerical score to each feature [13, 82], indicating its importance in the classification of each instance. Among these, SHAP [57] provides a theoretically grounded measure of feature importance based on Shapley values [92], and has been recently extended to support time series data [62]. Other approaches focus on providing global explanations , approximating deep-learning classifiers with explainable models [26, 23] or by learning decision rules [44, 48, 21]. In the context of anomaly detection, the literature on interpretable approaches is rather limited. C Experiment Registry 

In this section we provide the complete registry of all 215 experiments in the dataset, grouped by anomaly type. The Details column summarizes key anomaly configuration parameters (onset time, duration, target components, etc.). The Notes column contains additional comments on the experiment, including any experimental artifacts that occured. Namely, permanent disk loss indicates 23 the target disk could not be successfully recovered and stayed offline for the remainder of the experiment; server metric loss indicates issues with the target’s server telemetry due to software crashes; unplanned crash indicates the target server could not be restarted automatically at anomaly remediation time. rerun of ID indicates the experiment is a rerun of an experiment containing artifacts; the resulting time series from such experiment and its rerun are therefore very different. Experiment details use a compact notation summarized below. • Disk references follow the pattern sN /disk , where sN is the storage server number (1–3) and disk is the NVMe device name (e.g. g2disk01 ). Multiple disks are joined with +.• Timing is given as onset (time from experiment start to fault injection) and dur (fault duration). When every disk or node shares the same onset and duration, these appear once at the end of the entry; when they differ, the compact form @onset /dur is appended to each individual fault. Faults within a non-simultaneous entry are separated by semicolons. • Asynchronous patterns are prefixed with one of three labels: cascading (failures start close together and mostly overlap), rolling (sequential with partial overlap), or independent (no temporal overlap). • Server degradation entries list either max delay / loss for linear ramps, or a sequence of delay /loss @pct % stages for gradual (staircase) profiles. Delay is the added network latency; loss is the packet-drop percentage. Stage percentages refer to elapsed fractions of the full anomaly duration. • Software node (snode) counts are written as sN : k snode(s) , indicating k software storage nodes killed on server N. When durations differ per server, they appear inline after the snode count.

C.3 Server Failures 

Server failure experiments simulate the loss / degradation of an entire storage server. Because the warp S3 load balancer distributes requests across all three servers in round-robin, losing one server reduces overall throughput proportionally and changes the traffic shape seen by the remaining servers. 

Single instant failures drop all network traffic on the target server. Asynchronous instant failures apply the same mechanism to multiple servers at different times within a single experiment (two servers cannot be killed simultaneously). Degradation experiments inject progressive network impairment in two ways: linear ramps delay and packet loss smoothly from zero to configured maximums over the anomaly duration, while gradual applies them in discrete stages at configured percentages of the anomaly duration, creating a staircase pattern. 28 D Technical Details 

D.1 System Details 

The cluster topology shared by all experimental deployments is illustrated in Figure 12. Each cluster runs RING 9.5.0.5 on top of Rocky Linux 9.5 with 3 storage servers and one supervisor server. Each storage server is equipped with four 10 GB NVMe data disks and two 5 GB SSDs for metadata, in addition to one OS disk. Client S3 traffic generated by warp on the supervisor enters each storage server through its S3 connector service. The connector parses S3 API calls, performs metadata lookups, and dispatches data operations to the appropriate storage nodes via the DHT. Warp distributes client requests to all three servers in round-robin, before further load balancing is done at the connector level. The RING’s data placement in each storage server is governed by an implementation of the Chord protocol [79], a peer-to-peer distributed hash table in which object keys are hashed and mapped onto a logical ring of identifiers. Each of the 18 software storage nodes (6 per server) owns a contiguous arc of this keyspace and is responsible for storing all objects whose hashes fall within its range. Chord ensures that when a node joins or leaves the ring, only the keyspace segments adjacent to it are affected, as the protocol redistributes the affected key ranges to their new owners efficiently. Therefore, when storage nodes are killed, or servers are isolated, the remaining nodes absorb the orphaned keyspace segments and continue serving requests. Objects are protected via replication or erasure coding depending on size, with replicas placed on distinct storage nodes across different servers for durability. Three categories of background tasks run continuously and are directly relevant to the anomaly experiments: rebuild detects missing replicas or chunks (e.g., after a disk or node failure) and reconstructs them onto healthy disks; repair scans for data integrity issues and fixes them from surviving copies; and rebalance redistributes data when disk usage becomes uneven, typically after a recovery event. These tasks run at low intensity during nominal operation and ramp up after failure events, producing visible signatures in the per-server task-rate metrics (see Table 7).

D.2 Implementation Details Baseline workload generation: S3 client traffic is generated by warp v1.0.8 3, an open-source S3 benchmarking tool. Warp runs on the supervisor server and distributes requests to all three storage servers via round-robin host selection ( --host-select roundrobin ). The tool is invoked in stress mode ( --stress ) to discard output for long-running experiments, and object sizes are randomized within the configured range ( --obj.randsize ). An important consequence of the 

> 3https://github.com/minio/warp

29 round-robin distribution is that, when a storage server becomes unreachable during a server failure experiment, approximately one-third of all S3 requests are dropped by the client, producing a visible and proportional throughput reduction across the cluster. 

Disk failure injection: Disk failures are triggered by fully detaching the target NVMe disk from the operating system. The framework resolves the disk’s mount point to its underlying block device and PCI bus address, then performs a lazy unmount and unbinds the NVMe driver via the kernel’s 

sysfs interface. This removes the block device entirely, simulating physical hardware loss rather than merely blocking I/O. Recovery rebinds the NVMe driver, waits for the device to reappear, marks the disk as out-of-service via the platform’s disk management utility, wipes its partition signatures, and initiates a RING-level disk replacement. The RING then begins background rebuild tasks to reconstruct the lost replicas on healthy disks. In a subset of simultaneous disk failure experiments, the replacement procedure failed silently and the target disk remained permanently offline for the rest of the experiment. These “permanent disk loss” cases are annotated in the experiment registry (Appendix C, Table 12) and represent a realistic failure mode. To mitigate this in other experiments, a staggered recovery strategy was adopted, where disks on the same server are fixed with slight time offsets to reduce contention during the replacement procedure. 

Server failure injection: All server failure modes use Linux tc (traffic control) with the netem 

network emulation module to manipulate network traffic at the kernel level. SSH and ARP traffic is explicitly exempted from impairment throughout, preserving the experiment framework’s remote management access to the target server. In instant mode, all non-SSH traffic is subjected to 100% packet loss, effectively isolating the server from the rest of the cluster. In linear degradation mode, network delay and packet loss are gradually ramped from zero to their configured maximum values over the anomaly duration. In gradual (staged) degradation mode, delay and packet loss are applied in discrete steps at configured percentage milestones of the anomaly duration, producing a staircase pattern. Recovery reverses all network impairments and restores the kernel’s default queueing disciplines. Two server failure experiments experienced genuine unplanned server crashes caused by the stress of the injected anomaly, requiring manual recovery after a much longer outage than planned. Both were subsequently re-run with shorter, controlled durations. The original and re-run experiments are both included in the dataset and annotated in the experiment registry (Appendix C, Table 13). 

Storage node failure injection: Storage node failures target individual software processes on a given server. The experiment framework randomly selects the configured number of targets (1–3 out of the 6 running per server) and terminates them with SIGABRT (signal 6), producing a core dump that mimics a real application crash. Recovery restarts each killed node via the platform’s process management wrapper, after which the RING initiates background rebuild and repair tasks to restore the node’s data. A key distinction from server failures is that the storage server itself remains reachable: its remaining storage nodes continue to handle requests normally, and only the DHT keyspace segments owned by the killed nodes become temporarily unavailable while the Chord protocol redistributes their workload. 16 early storage node experiments were affected by a telemetry agent bug: killing a storage node process caused an unhandled connection error in the server’s local telemetry agent, which disrupted metric collection for all services on the affected server. The agent was subsequently patched, and the experiments were re-run. Both versions are included in the dataset, as they produce distinct telemetry signatures, and are annotated accordingly in the experiment registry (Appendix C, Table 14). 

Experiment setup: Internally, experiments are defined declaratively as YAML configuration files specifying the workload parameters and a timeline of fault injection events, each with an absolute time offset from experiment start, a configured duration and subsequent anomaly-specific parameters. A distribution of anomaly parameter coverage across all experiments can be found in Table 15. The orchestrator schedules each timeline event at its specified offset from experiment start. Anomalies are automatically reversed after their configured duration. Subsequently, Mahalanobis distance is utilized to calculate the distance from sub-sequences to the mean, which is regarded as the anomaly score. Anomaly Transformer [98] AT S Prediction-based Utilizes the ‘Anomaly-Attention’ mechanism to compute the association discrepancy. KMeansAD [100] KMAD U Distance-based calculates the anomaly scores for each sub-sequence by measuring the distance to the centroid of its assigned cluster, as determined by the k-means algorithm. PCA [5] PCA U Density-based Projects data to a lower-dimensional hyperplane, with significant deviation from this plane indicating potential outliers. CBLOF [34] CBLOF U Distance-based Cluster-based LOF, which calculates the anomaly score by first assigning samples to clusters, and then using the distance among clusters as anomaly scores. KNN [43] KNN U Distance-based Produces the anomaly score of the input instance as the distance to its k-th nearest neighbor. HBOS [31] HBOS U Density-based Constructs a histogram for the data and uses the inverse of the height of the bin as the anomaly score of the data point. LOF [19] LOF U Distance-based Calculates the anomaly score by comparing local density with that of its neighbors. IsolationForest [53] IF U Density-based Isolates anomalies using random partitioning of the data space. The core assumption is that anomalies are easier to isolate, requiring fewer random splits. Therefore, in-stances that require fewer partitions are more likely to be anomalous. Robust PCA [64] RPCA U Density-based Built upon PCA, identifies anomalies by recovering the principal matrix.

For the interpretability experiments, we rely on a large language model accessed via API. These timings may vary depending on external factors such as API load and network conditions, but provide a reasonable estimate of the computational overhead associated with LLM-based interpretation in our setting.

F Limitations 

One of the limitations of SHAD is that each time series considers one type of anomalies, thereby not providing series with mixed failures (e.g. a server failure followed by a disk failure). Moreover, SHAD currently focuses on a single industrial distributed storage system. This design choice was made to prioritize annotation quality and reliability through close collaboration with domain experts, at the cost of reduced system heterogeneity. Extending the benchmark to additional storage systems and application domains is an important direction for future work, although it requires access to real 35 Table 21: Mean gain by supervision level and task.

For the explainability experiment, we considered a traditional explainability method to understand whether simple dimension score attribution could be used to correctly identify anomalous dimensions. The evaluation of more refined strategies, which proved to be necessary in light of the results, is therefore left as future development. In addition, for evaluating interpretability, we provide a simple baseline consisting in a single-call solution. While more refined strategies (e.g., per-dimension analysis, advanced prompting, or multi-stage reasoning) or tailored approaches (e.g., ChatTS) can be used, the purpose of our experiments is to establish a simple baseline from which future work on LLM-based anomaly interpretation can build. 

G Broader Impacts 

SHAD provides a comprehensive benchmark for time series anomaly detection, explanation, and interpretation on real-world, high-dimensional telemetry data from cloud storage systems. By en-abling rigorous and reproducible evaluation of detection, explainability, and interpretability methods, SHAD can contribute to the development of more reliable and transparent monitoring systems. This may improve the robustness and availability of critical digital infrastructures (e.g., cloud services), leading to reduced downtime, better resource utilization, and improved user experience. Furthermore, the inclusion of per-dimension anomaly labels and rich textual annotations facilitates research on interpretable machine learning, helping practitioners better understand system failures and supporting faster debugging and root-cause analysis. This can reduce operational costs and lower the barrier to adopting advanced anomaly detection techniques in industry. At the same time, models developed and evaluated exclusively using SHAD may exhibit limited generalization if applied to domains with substantially different characteristics, and anomaly detection systems may still produce false positives or missed detections in practice. Additionally, advances in system observability could, in certain contexts, be leveraged to infer sensitive operational patterns or to design strategies that evade detection. These considerations depend on specific deployment settings and highlight the importance of careful evaluation, domain-specific validation, and responsible use. 36
