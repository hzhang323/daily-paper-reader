# Abstract 

Outcome-based reinforcement learning can train language models to forecast real-world events, but prior forecasting work either freezes research context before training or deploys agentic research only at test time, so the skill of gathering 

evidence is never shaped by the reward. We introduce an agentic forecasting environment, dataset, and harness built from 2,100+ resolved Polymarket questions; the agent acquires its own context at rollout time (web search, page reading, and financial time series, all restricted by layered leak filtering to information published before each question’s cutoff), and we train Qwen3.5-35B-A3B (3B active parameters) on it with single-epoch GRPO under a Brier-score reward. Training changes how the agent interacts with information: calibration improves 30–40%, and search attempts fall from 3.8 to 2.25 per rollout as evidence discipline is learned. Evaluated in an identical harness against four frontier models, the trained policy also finishes ahead of every frontier model tested at evidence-based forecasting, including Claude Opus 4.5 (soft-Brier 0.254 vs. 0.256, n=265 ), at about 5% of the inference cost, and its margin is widest on the hardest questions, the ones the crowd itself had not decided. We release the environment, dataset, and per-rollout records as a reusable harness for temporal forecasting agents. 1

# 1 Introduction 

Prediction markets such as Polymarket let traders buy and sell contracts on real-world events; every question therefore carries a continuously updated crowd probability (the market price) and, once the event resolves, a verified outcome; few other domains give agentic RL verifiable, dated answers at this scale. Two recent lines of work showed that outcome-based RL on these questions produces strong forecasters. Turtel et al. (2025) train a 14B model with outcome-only RL on 10k resolved questions plus 100k synthetic ones, reaching statistical parity with o1 on accuracy while halving its calibration error; Jeen et al. (2026) fine-tune gpt-oss-120b on ∼10 k questions to a Metaculus baseline score marginally above Gemini 3 Pro, and find that research context drives most of the training gain (+7 points with it, +3 without). Both freeze the context before training, pasted headlines in the former, a pre-generated “research phase” in the latter, leaving results bounded by the quality of that one-time collection. Turtel et al. (2025) attribute part of their remaining gap to the information lag of frozen headlines, and Jeen et al. (2026) acknowledge that frozen context caps performance, since no policy improvement can recover information the prompt lacks, naming the follow-up outright: “Folding retrieval into the training loop is the natural way to test this.” On the other hand, Murphy (2026) makes forecasting agentic at test time by introducing a multi-turn harness in which a frozen model sequentially updates an explicit belief state through iterative tool use. Even optimized retrieval scaffolds, however, only approach crowd accuracy (Halawi et al., 2024); without non-public

We close the frozen-context gap by folding a multi-turn search-and-forecast scaffold directly into the RL training loop; RL with verifiable rewards has already incentivized reasoning, most prominently in DeepSeek-R1 (Guo et al., 2025), and search, as in Search-R1 (Jin et al., 2025); the enabling step for forecasting is making it temporally sound on resolved historical questions. At rollout time, the agent decides, over multiple turns, what to search, which pages to read, and which time series to pull, with layered leak filtering restricting every channel to information published before the question’s cutoff. Bringing context acquisition into the RL loop under the same verifiable reward moves the field from optimizing a single-turn output to training a multi-turn reasoning forecaster, allowing the agent to improve its search queries, weigh how far to trust each result, and learn which evidence bears on the outcome. In contrast to time-series foundation models (Ansari et al., 2024; Das et al., 2024), which forecast from a fixed numeric context window, our agent chooses its own context at rollout time, numeric series included, and context acquisition itself becomes the learned temporal skill. Our contributions: 1. An agentic forecasting dataset, environment, and harness (open-sourced on the Prime Intellect Environments Hub): 2,113 training and 265 held-out resolved Polymarket questions with strict temporal eligibility, as well as the harness configuration and code to allow multi-turn agentic forecasting in both training and testing. 2. An identical-harness evaluation of interactive forecasting agents : trained, untrained, and four frontier models run with the same tools, filters, and turn budget, with platform-served policies, per-rollout capture, and trace-level audits as part of the method. 3. # 2 The environment and dataset 

Task and harness. Each episode presents one resolved binary Polymarket question with its resolu-tion criteria and a forecast cutoff date; the agent must produce a probability that the event occurs, using only information available before the cutoff. It runs a multi-turn tool loop (up to 10 turns): web search ( ≤3 calls, served through an Amazon Bedrock AgentCore gateway), URL reading with LLM summarization, financial and economic time series truncated at the cutoff, revision-dated Wikipedia access, a trend-extrapolation tool, and a terminal submit action returning a probability and rationale. The system prompt requests an explicit belief state (Murphy, 2026): every tool call is meant to carry an updated probability and evidence lists, and each prediction has a human-readable reasoning trace, an auditable update trajectory from prior to final probability, and an inspectable world-state model. 

Leak filtering. Because questions are historical, the central validity threat is information from after the cutoff leaking into the rollout. Retrieved content therefore passes three layers before the model sees it: a domain blocklist (prediction-market sites and known mirrors are never fetched); a heuristic date filter that parses publication dates, drops post-cutoff results, and scrubs lines containing post-cutoff facts; and a per-result keep/drop judgment by Claude Haiku (via Amazon Bedrock) against the question and cutoff, which catches undated pages and indirect outcome reveals. Haiku alone scored perfectly among five judges benchmarked on labeled keep/drop cases (the others: Gemma-3-12B, gpt-oss-20b, gpt-oss-safeguard-20b, and Nova 2 Lite); every alternative erred toward over-dropping valid evidence rather than leaking outcomes (per-model results in the repository). 

Dataset. Questions are drawn from the public Polymarket APIs and filtered for liquidity (volume 

≥$5 ,000 ), genuine undecidedness (market price at the cutoff in [0 .10 , 0.90] : the crowd had not effectively settled the question), and forecast horizon (cutoffs drawn hash-stably 7–90 days before resolution). Every cutoff and resolution postdates the base model’s release window, precluding any outcome from entering its parametric knowledge; test questions resolve strictly after all train-ing questions for backtesting compatibility. The Brier score is strictly proper (Savage, 1971; Gneiting and Raftery, 2007): truthful probabilities are uniquely optimal; it is bounded, giving low-variance policy gradients (Jeen et al., 2026); and it matches prior conven-tions (Turtel et al., 2025). The policy is trained with the market-price tools hidden; its only paths to reward are research and priors. The environment is built on verifiers ; training is single-epoch Group Relative Policy Optimization (GRPO) (Shao et al., 2024) with LoRA (33 steps × 64 questions 

× 8 rollouts) on Qwen3.5-35B-A3B, a sparse mixture-of-experts model with 3B active parameters, via prime-rl on Prime Intellect’s stack. 

# 3 Evaluation setup 

All policies (trained, untrained base, Claude Opus 4.5, Claude Sonnet 4.5, Gemini 3.1 Pro, and Gemini 3.6 Flash) run the identical environment: same tools, same leak filters, same turn and search budgets. The Qwen policies are served by the training platform’s own inference stack and captured per-rollout via a results webhook; frontier models run the same environment through an API proxy. We report soft-Brier, the mean Brier score with 0.25 (the score of an uninformative 0.5 forecast) imputed whenever a policy declines to answer, and expected calibration error (ECE) over submitted forecasts with ten equal-mass bins (the gap between stated confidence and empirical frequency), following Turtel et al. (2025); both are lower-is-better. All evaluations use live retrieval with no access to the market’s own price; we call this evidence-based forecasting . Price access makes every model, from the untrained 35B to Opus 4.5, converge on the crowd without reliably beating it, so a market-visible comparison measures price-copying, not forecasting (Halawi et al., 2024; Appendix A). 

# 4 Results 

Beating the frontier. Figure 1 gives the headline result: the RL-trained policy finishes ahead of every frontier model tested, edging Claude Opus 4.5 (0.254 vs. 0.256) and leading Sonnet, Gemini Pro, and Flash by 0.019–0.032. A 3B-active open model, trained for one epoch on ∼2,100 questions, tops the frontier at about 5% of the inference cost. 2 The trained model also posts the lowest calibration error of the panel (Figure 2); submission rates are 99–100% throughout, except Gemini Pro at 94%.

Margins widen in low-confidence markets. We pre-declared a subset of n=104 questions with a cutoff price in [0 .30 , 0.70] , those the crowd itself had not yet decided, where the trained advantage concentrates (Figure 1). While every score is higher on this harder subset (the crowd itself scores 0.232), the margins widen. The trained policy (0.274) leads Opus by 0.007 (0.281), Sonnet by 0.021 (0.295), and Pro and Flash by 0.042–0.045 (0.316, 0.319); every paired per-question gap is larger than on the full set, and the Opus gap grows from a near-tie to 0.007. When the outcome is obvious, every competent researcher finds it and the forecasters converge; when the market is uncertain, forecasts rest on each agent’s own research and intuition, and the benefit of training is reaped. 

Calibration and coverage improve. Training improves soft-Brier over the untrained base in every setting we measured, though its largest and most reliable gains are in calibration and coverage (submission rate; Figure 3). ECE falls 0.185 →0.128 ( −31% ) in the evidence-based setting, placing the trained model ahead of the frontier panel (Figure 2), while coverage rises from 79% to ∼100% 

(+26% ). Search attempts also fall from 3.8 to 2.25 per rollout: the trained policy re-queries less after empty or filtered results and reasons more from the surviving evidence. This 30–40% calibration improvement holds in every train/eval pair of the repository’s variant runs (0.099 →0.065 with the market price visible; 0.170 →0.103 in a search-off, no-market run). Rollout traces show this discipline directly (examples in Appendix B). 

# 5 Conclusion 

While outcome-based RL in prediction-market environments has evidently improved the forecasting priors of models, previous approaches are scaffold-dependent and context-sensitive. Internalizing context acquisition, training the act of research itself, removes this extrinsic sensitivity: agents become intrinsically better forecasters. By teaching a 35B model how to conduct its own research, we elevate both its parametric accuracy and behavioral discipline to frontier levels, establishing a more representative and fair way to train and evaluate forecasting agents. In short, the model learns to forecast by learning to search, and, just as importantly, when not to: search attempts fall from 3.8 to 2.25 per rollout even as accuracy rises and calibration error falls 31%. Future work should explore scaling this environment beyond binary prediction markets to categorical outcomes, as well as expanding the action space to allow agents to run their own sandboxed quantitative models. 4Acknowledgments and Disclosure of Funding

# A Why the market price is withheld 

With market-price tools available, every policy converges toward the crowd (soft-Brier band 0.186– 0.215 vs. crowd 0.189) and none reliably beats it. Withholding the price costs each policy its 

anchor-worth , the paired Brier cost of losing the crowd’s probability, measured on the same questions: 

+0 .055 to +0 .075 for the frontier models and +0 .04 for the 35B policies (trained and base), several times any training effect we measured. Comparisons between agents can therefore be reversed by choosing who sees the price (Figure 4).
