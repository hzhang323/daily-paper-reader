## ABSTRACT 

Multivariate Time Series Forecasting (MTSF) models that mix information across channels assume that the past of one channel carries information about the fu-ture of another. Yet they are evaluated on a small fixed set of standard datasets whose cross-channel structure is rarely examined. We ask two questions: How can we reliably measure lagged, non-linear, and joint coupling in MTSF datasets? 

and Do the standard datasets actually have such coupling? To answer the first, we test four candidate measures on synthetic datasets with planted ground-truth coupling: Granger Causality (GC), Transfer Entropy (TE), lagged Mutual Infor-mation (MI), and the CD gain, a model-based measure we introduce that com-pares a channel-dependent (CD) model to its channel-independent (CI) variant. Only lagged MI and the CD gain recover every planted coupling. For the sec-ond question, the answer is a definite no , as the standard datasets have a me-dian of only 23% lagged-coupled channel pairs and a median CD gain of −4.9% ,compared to 78% and +4 .6% on chaotic ODE systems. We therefore propose 

MixBench-TS , a benchmark of 10 real-world datasets with a median of 55.5% lagged-coupled pairs and a median CD gain of +1 .7% . Across six state-of-the-art models tuned under one protocol, CI models win on 10/10 (MSE) and 8/10 (MAE) standard datasets, but on only 3/10 and 2/10 MixBench-TS datasets. We recommend using our benchmark for evaluating new CD models. More-over, we propose profiling new datasets with lagged MI and the CD gain be-fore using them to evaluate multivariate models. Code and data are available at 

https://anonymous.4open.science/r/mixbench-ts-B027 .

## 1 INTRODUCTION 

Multivariate Time Series Forecasting (MTSF) models that mix information across channels are de-signed on an assumption of lagged coupling: a source channel’s past carries additional information about a target channel’s future . Contemporaneous coupling alone is not enough, since the source’s value at the timestep of forecasting is unknown as well. On datasets whose cross-channel coupling is weak or mostly contemporaneous, a channel-dependent (CD) model therefore gains nothing over a channel-independent (CI) model, which forecasts each channel from its own past. Such datasets are a not a good fit for evaluating CD models. Prior work has characterized MTSF datasets along axes such as domain coverage and temporal prop-erties (Godahewa et al., 2021; Qiu et al., 2024), but not along the lagged coupling that CD models rely on. The closest effort (Abdelmalak et al., 2026) uses ODE datasets from Gilpin (2021) to argue that current datasets lack channel coupling. However, it relies on Granger Causality (GC)(Granger, 1969), which cannot capture non-linear lagged coupling. Another limitation is that they consider only the standard and ODE datasets. This leaves two open questions: Q1. How can we reliably measure lagged, non-linear, and joint coupling in MTSF datasets?

Q2. Once measured, do the standard datasets used by the field (Lai et al., 2018; Zhou et al., 2021; Wu et al., 2021) actually have such coupling? 

To address Q1, we curate six synthetic datasets with known planted couplings. On this suite, we test four candidate measures: Granger Causality (GC; Granger, 1969), Transfer Entropy (TE; Schreiber, 2000), lagged Mutual Information ( MI lag ; Shannon, 1948; Cover & Thomas, 2006), and the CD gain ( CD g ), a model-based measure we introduce that compares a CD forecaster to its CI variant, following the landmarking concept of Pfahringer et al. (2000). GC misses the non-linear and joint couplings, and TE places the contemporaneous coupling at a wrong, positive lag. Only MI lag and 

CD g recover every planted coupling (Table 1), so we adopt them as our two profiling tools. We then address Q2 by measuring MI lag and CD g on the 10 standard datasets, using six datasets from the chaotic ODE benchmark (Gilpin, 2021) as a known-correlated reference. The answer is a definite no , as the standard datasets show weak lagged coupling compared to the ODE datasets (median of 23% vs. 78% lagged-coupled pairs, Figure 1 and Table 3). Moreover, a CI variant of TSMixer beats the full channel-mixing model on all 10 standard datasets, while channel mixing helps on every ODE dataset (median CD g of −4.9% vs. +4 .6% ). If the standard datasets fall short, the natural next step is to find datasets that have the lagged coupling that channel mixing needs. We therefore propose MixBench-TS , a benchmark of 10 real-world datasets that exist in the literature but are rarely used for MTSF evaluation (Qiu et al., 2024; Qiao et al., 2026; Stein et al., 2025; Baigutanova et al., 2025), selected by their positive CD g . They cover environmental monitoring, energy, hydrology, marine sensing, and health, and we refer to them throughout as the coupled datasets . They show substantially stronger lagged coupling than the standard datasets. To test whether this pattern carries over to actual forecasting, we evaluate six state-of-the-art models spanning both channel strategies (CI and CD), tuned under one unified protocol that also covers the lookback window (Abdelmalak et al., 2026). On the coupled datasets, where lagged coupling is substantially stronger, CI models win on only 3 of 10 (MSE), compared to all 10 standard datasets. Our contributions are as follows: • We introduce the CD gain ( CD g ), a model-based measure of exploitable cross-channel coupling in MTSF datasets, and validate it alongside three other candidate measures on synthetic datasets with known ground-truth coupling (Sections 4 and 5). 2Preprint. Under review. • We show that the datasets the field treats as standard are poor tests of CD models (Sec-tion 6). • We propose MixBench-TS, a benchmark of 10 real-world coupled datasets that are rarely used for MTSF evaluation and carry substantially stronger lagged coupling than the stan-dard datasets (Section 7.1). • We benchmark six models under a unified tuning protocol and show that the CI advantage collapses on the coupled datasets (Section 7.3). 1

## 2 RELATED WORK 

Channel mixing strategies in MTSF models. There has been rising interest in building MTSF models with varying architectures. We categorize recent models into CI and CD models based on whether a model treats each channel as a separate time series or mixes information across chan-nels. The CI category covers linear (Zeng et al., 2023), MLP-based (Lin et al., 2024), and even transformer-based models that still operate on each channel in isolation (Nie et al., 2023). The CD category includes transformer-based models that apply attention across channels (Zhang & Yan, 2023; Liu et al., 2024; Chen et al., 2025a) and MLP-based or linear models that mix channels (Chen et al., 2023; Yue et al., 2025). It also covers CNN-based models (Luo & Wang, 2024) and frequency-domain-based methods (Wang et al., 2024; 2025). More recent CD models mix channels selectively rather than fully, using graph-based filtering (Hu et al., 2025) or channel clustering (Qiu et al., 2025; Chen et al., 2024). 

Benchmarks and evaluation. Many recent papers have extended the datasets available for MTSF in different directions, such as ODE-based benchmarks (Gilpin, 2021; Kl¨ otergens et al., 2025), bursty network-traffic benchmarks (Guthula et al., 2025), causality-driven benchmarks (Stein et al., 2025), and application-traffic benchmarks balanced across forecasting regimes (Xue et al., 2026). Other benchmarks have been proposed to extend domain coverage and characterize temporal prop-erties of the data (Qiu et al., 2024; Godahewa et al., 2021; Aksu et al., 2024). On the evaluation 

side, Zeng et al. (2023) show that a simple linear model outperforms transformers on the standard datasets. Meanwhile, Chen et al. (2025b) analyze the existing benchmark based on transformer-based models’ performance. Roque et al. (2025) investigate how papers choose datasets to evaluate on, while Brigato et al. (2026) study the effect of hyperparameter tuning and forecasting horizons on model performance on the standard datasets. Closer to our work, some studies investigate when cross-channel modeling pays off. For instance, Han et al. (2024) attribute the strong performance of CI models to their robustness to distribution shift. Shao et al. (2025) link model performance to the level of heterogeneity within datasets. Finally, Abdelmalak et al. (2026) show via Granger Causality that the standard datasets carry little linear channel coupling. We build on this literature by following rigorous evaluation practices in our protocol, and by extending the analysis to non-linear and lagged coupling. 

## 3 PROBLEM FORMULATION 

Each dataset is a multivariate time series Z = ( z1, . . . , zT ) ∈ RT ×C with C channels (variates) observed at T equally spaced steps. Following Qiu et al. (2024), Z is split chronologically into training, validation, and test segments, and samples are built with a sliding window of stride 1. A model fθ : RL×C → RH×C maps a lookback window of the last L steps to the next H steps (the 

forecast horizon ), jointly over all C channels. Forecast targets never cross a split boundary (Ap-pendix C). The standard datasets commonly use H ∈ { 96 , 192 , 336 , 720 }, while for the remaining datasets we adapt the horizons to be semantically relevant (Appendix A).

Each pair is jointly made stationary (seasonal and ADF-selected regular differencing, Said & Dickey, 1984), so GC reflects directed predictive structure rather than shared trend or seasonality. Transfer Entropy (TE). TE uses the same joint differencing as GC, so the linear and non-linear measures see identical series. It is the directed, non-linear generalization of GC, approximated by the rank-space conditional mutual information I(yt; xt−ℓ | yt−1) (Schreiber, 2000; Kraskov et al., 2004). Here, yt is the target’s present, xt−ℓ the source’s value ℓ steps earlier, and yt−1 the target’s most recent past. As in the restricted GC model, conditioning on yt−1 removes what the target’s own past already explains, so only the additional information carried by the source is counted. We sweep the lags ℓ ∈ { 1, . . . , p }, with p as for GC, and select ℓ⋆ as the statistically significant lag with the largest effective value (Appendix D). The statistical measures above test for specific forms of coupling (linear, non-linear, lagged) between pairs of channels, and can therefore miss coupling that only emerges jointly across several channels. ## 5 EVALUATION OF CHANNEL DEPENDENCY MEASUREMENTS 

5.1 SYNTHETIC DATASETS CURATION 

To check whether the four measures capture couplings of varying types, we build synthetic datasets with known ground-truth coupling, which allows us to verify whether each measure recovers it. The datasets span three classes of cross-channel coupling: (i) no coupling, (ii) pairwise coupling, where a single source channel drives a target channel, either contemporaneously or with a lag and either linearly or non-linearly, and (iii) joint coupling, where several source channels drive the same target channel. Each base channel ci is an Autoregressive (AR) process of order pi driven by indepen-dent Gaussian noise, plus a seasonal component with a channel-specific period. We then plant a coupling from one or more source channels into a target channel c0, according to its coupling type: 

coupling ∈ { contemp-linear , lagged-linear , lagged-non-linear , multiplicative-joint , additive-mixed }.Figure 2 shows the six datasets and their planted coupling structure. The independent dataset estab-lishes the null values of the measures. The three pairwise datasets couple a single source c1 into c0,and the two joint datasets couple c1 and c2 into c0. In multiplicative-joint , the target depends on the product of both sources, so neither source affects it on its own. ## 7 THE MIX BENCH -TS B ENCHMARK 

7.1 DATASETS 

We propose MixBench-TS, a benchmark of 10 real-world datasets collected from recent MTSF benchmarks and public data repositories. We refer to these datasets as the coupled datasets .AQShunyi, CzeLan, and ZafNoo are taken from TFB (Qiu et al., 2024), WaterQuality-Darwin and Current-Velocity from TIME (Qiao et al., 2026), and Bavaria-Iller and Bavaria-Donau from Causal-Rivers (Stein et al., 2025). Wearable is taken from Baigutanova et al. (2025), AirQuality from the Beijing multi-site air-quality data (Zhang et al., 2017), and HouseholdPower from the UCI house-hold power consumption data (Hebrail & Berard, 2006). Although publicly available, these datasets are rarely used for MTSF evaluation. Candidate lookback windows and forecast horizons are chosen per dataset based on its sampling interval (Appendix A). ## 8 CONCLUSIONS AND FUTURE WORK 

In this work, we asked two questions. First, how can we reliably measure lagged, non-linear, and joint coupling in MTSF datasets? On synthetic datasets with known ground-truth coupling, only lagged MI ( MI lag ) and CD gain ( CD g ) retrieve all planted couplings. Second, do the standard MTSF datasets have such coupling? The answer is no. They have a median of only 23% lagged-coupled channel pairs compared to 78% in the chaotic ODE benchmark, and channel mixing hurts on every one of them. We therefore proposed MixBench-TS, a benchmark of 10 real-world coupled datasets for evaluating multivariate forecasting models. These datasets have a median of 55.5% lagged-coupled pairs and a positive CD g , and CI models win on only 3 of them, compared to all 10 standard datasets. We recommend profiling further datasets with MI lag and CD g . Those with 

CD g > 0 can join MixBench-TS, and those that also have MI lag > 50% can join MixBench-TS-Core. Evaluating multivariate models requires benchmarks that actually contain multivariate structure, so that the benefits of channel mixing can be observed rather than assumed. 

Limitations. First, CD g uses a single model family (TSMixer) and thus also reflects whether this architecture can exploit the coupling, e.g., Electricity has 61% lagged pairs but a CD g of −5.1% .Second, MI lag is a pairwise fraction that ignores coupling strength, captures joint coupling only indirectly, and uses at most 40 channels on wide datasets. Third, our synthetic suite has only four channels and fixed couplings, without the regime changes and hidden common drivers of real data. 

Future Work. The landmarking concept behind CD g can be extended beyond TSMixer to further CD models and their CI variants, making CD g less dependent on a single architecture. Per-channel and time-windowed coupling maps could guide selective channel mixing and capture couplings that change across regimes. Finally, our criteria can broaden MixBench-TS with short-and-wide, spatio-temporal, and irregularly sampled datasets. 9Preprint. Under review.

## D STATISTICAL PROFILING - I MPLEMENTATION DETAILS 

In this appendix, we provide more detailed implementation details which should be helpful in both reproducing the results and understanding our design choices for these metrics. For datasets with more than 40 channels, all three pairwise measures are computed on a subset of 40 channels drawn by uniform random sampling without replacement. A sensitivity analysis over this subsampling is reported in Appendix G. D.1 GRANGER CAUSALITY 

Granger Causality was originally proposed by Granger (1969) and has been widely used in econo-metrics and time series analysis. The basic idea is that if a time series X Granger-causes another time series Y , then past values of X should contain information that helps predict Y beyond the in-formation contained in past values of Y alone. What makes this method more challenging to apply in practice are the following factors: • Stationarity : Granger Causality assumes that the time series are stationary. In practice, many real-world time series exhibit trends and seasonality, which can violate this assump-tion. To address this, we apply seasonal differencing and regular differencing to make the series stationary before applying Granger Causality tests. • Lag Selection : The choice of lag length is crucial in Granger Causality analysis. For comparability with deep learning models, we set the VAR order p equal to the smallest candidate lookback window of the dataset (the order p of the main text). This allows us to capture the relevant temporal dependencies without introducing unnecessary complexity. The seasonal period P is detected once per dataset, before any channel subsampling, as the strongest local peak of the autocorrelation function averaged over all detrended channels. It therefore does not depend on the channel subset, and both series of a pair receive the same seasonal difference at period 

P . For the multi-series panels, we use a fixed period instead ( P = 96 for WaterQuality-Darwin and 

P = 72 for Current-Velocity), and the synthetic lag profiles use P = 24 . The seasonal difference is skipped if the series is shorter than 3P . We then use the Augmented Dickey-Fuller (ADF) (Said & Dickey, 1984) test to determine if additional regular differencing is needed. The ADF test checks for the presence of a unit root in the time series, which indicates non-stationarity. For each pair, the ADF test selects a differencing order d ≤ 2 separately for the source and the target, and both series are then differenced max( dsrc , d tgt ) times. This common order keeps the two series aligned and equally transformed. With these settings fixed, both models are fitted with Ordinary Least Squares (OLS) on the pre-processed series. The sums of squared residuals ( SSR ) of the restricted and multivariate models are then used to compute the degrees-of-freedom corrected partial R2 (Geweke linear feedback (Geweke, 1982)), which quantifies the linear directed predictability of one time series from another. As with TE, this per-pair R2 is aggregated to a single dataset value by taking the median over all directed channel pairs, as reported in the main text. D.2 TRANSFER ENTROPY (TE) Now regarding the Transfer Entropy, we use the same joint differencing as Granger Causality, so the linear and non-linear axes see identical series. The main idea of TE is to measure the amount of directed information between two time series, capturing non-linear coupling that GC might miss. TE is computed based on KSG estimator (Kraskov et al., 2004) quantifying the conditional mutual information between the present state of one time series and the past state of another, conditioned on the target’s own most recent value. Conditioning on a single past value keeps the conditioning set low-dimensional, which the k-nearest-neighbor estimator requires to remain reliable. Computing the CMI on the ranks of the series (rather than their raw values) makes the estimate invariant to 17 Preprint. Under review. monotone transformations and robust to outliers, while the non-parametric estimator captures non-linear coupling of any form. Unlike GC, TE does not directly produce a p-value, so we use a surrogate data approach to generate a null distribution of TE values. Following Runge (2018), we cast TE as the conditional independence test X ⊥ Y | Z with source X = xt−ℓ, target Y = yt, and conditioning set Z = yt−1 (the target’s own past). Rather than a full random shuffle, the surrogates are then drawn with a local-permutation scheme: each source sample is permuted only among its 

kperm nearest neighbors in the conditioning space Z, which breaks the X → Y information flow while preserving the marginal distributions and the dependence on Z. By comparing the observed TE value to this null distribution, we can assess the statistical significance of the detected information flow. The test has a few essential parameters, which we set as follows: • TE Max Lag ( ℓTE max ): We set the maximum lag ℓTE max for TE computation to be equal to the smallest candidate lookback window of the dataset (the order p of GC). This is done to make sure that every coupling identified by TE is relevant to the forecasting task and not just a coupling in a lag that the model is not able to use. • Number of Surrogates : We generate 100 surrogate time series for each pair of time series to create a robust null distribution. This number is taken directly from the literature (Runge, 2018) and is a good trade-off between computational cost and statistical power (as a higher number of surrogates does not yield higher stability in results anymore). • kCMI : The number of nearest neighbors used in the KSG estimator of the CMI. This value also followed the original paper’s rule of thumb of picking the k value based on the number of usable samples n in the dataset, kCMI = min(0 .2 n, 200) . Scaling k with n lowers the estimator’s variance on long series, and the cap bounds the computational cost. • kperm : The number of nearest neighbors (in the conditioning space Z) used by the local-permutation scheme for the surrogate null distribution. This is set universally for all datasets as 5, which was shown to work well for a variety of the datasets in the original paper (Runge, 2018). • Lag Sweep & Peak Selection ( ℓ⋆): For each ordered pair we evaluate the effective TE at every candidate lag ℓ ∈ { 1, . . . , ℓ TE max }. The reported value is the effective (debiased) transfer entropy TE eff = max  0, bI(yt; xt−ℓ | yt−1) − Inull

We test each swept lag against its surrogate null and apply a Benjamini–Hochberg false-discovery-rate correction across the swept lags (Benjamini & Hochberg, 1995). This controls the expected proportion of lags falsely flagged as significant. The reported lag ℓ⋆ is the FDR-significant lag with the largest effective TE, and the effective TE is set to 0 if no lag survives the correction. D.3 MUTUAL INFORMATION (MI) Now regarding the Mutual Information, unlike GC and TE it does not condition on the target’s own past. At lag 0, it is a symmetric measure of the overall coupling shared between a pair of channels. At positive lags, it is directional, from source to target, as in GC and TE. It also differs in preprocessing: MI is estimated on the raw series (rank-transformed for scale invariance, without 

the seasonal and regular differencing applied for GC and TE), so that shared trend and seasonality are retained as legitimate common-driver information rather than removed. The estimator itself is the same KSG estimator used for TE (Kraskov et al., 2004), and, as with TE, every MI estimate is debiased against a surrogate null (see the Number of Surrogates parameter below). We mainly use MI to estimate whether cross-channel coupling is lagged or contemporaneous. We set its parameters as follows: • kMI : The number of nearest neighbors used in the KSG estimator for MI computation. We set this value to 5, a common choice for the KSG estimator (Kraskov et al., 2004). 18 Preprint. Under review. • Number of Surrogates : Similar to TE, we generate 100 surrogate time series for each pair of channels to create a null distribution for the MI values, chosen at this value for consistency with TE. The surrogates are drawn by randomly permuting the source series, which destroys any coupling with the target while preserving its marginal distribution. Ev-ery reported MI value — both contemporaneous and lagged — is effective , i.e. debiased by subtracting the mean of this surrogate null from the raw KSG estimate. The peak-lag and lag-0 quantities that enter ϕlag are thus compared on the same debiased scale. • Lag Grid : We evaluate MI in two setups, contemporaneous and lagged. The contempo-raneous MI is computed between the present states of the two channels ( ℓ = 0 ), while the lagged MI is evaluated over the geometrically spaced candidate lags in Λ (Eq. 9) and retains the positive lag ℓ⋆ ∈ Λ that maximizes the effective MI. This is the lag reported in the main text. The selection is thus driven by the MI values on this fixed lag grid, not by any cross-correlation criterion.
