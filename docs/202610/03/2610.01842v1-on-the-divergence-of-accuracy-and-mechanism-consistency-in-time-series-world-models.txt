# On the Divergence of Accuracy and Mechanism Consistency in Time Series World Models 

Haochen Zhang 1, Jiaheng Guo 1, Zhen Xu 1, Zachary Plotkin 2, Nicholas Konz 1, Zhen Tan 3,Tianlong Chen 2 B

University of North Carolina at Chapel Hill 2Foci Labs 3Stevens Institute of Technology A time series world model (TSWM) takes the observed history of a controlled system and a plan of future actions and exogenous inputs, and predicts how the state will respond. Current approaches build such models as forecasters with the actions as covariates, trained and evaluated on prediction error under the executed plan. Yet a world model is used to compare plans that were never executed, so its response to a changed plan matters as much as its error, and this practice never tests it. Two questions are therefore open: which design choices matter, and whether an accurate forecaster responds to a changed plan the way the real system does. We address both with a formalization of TSWMs and a benchmark. The formalization separates state, actions and exogenous inputs, distinguishes continuous, mode and event actions, and introduces mechanism consistency, a metric built on declared mechanisms, relations between an action and a state variable whose direction is known, such as a vasopressor raising blood pressure: it checks whether shifting an action moves the forecast in the declared direction. The benchmark consolidates eight public datasets with real actions from engineered infrastructure and clinical care, and varies the prediction space, the plan fusion and the plan encoding across seven backbones and five seeds. First, a frozen latent prediction space lowers MAE by 9.9% over observation space and gated fusion at the output by 12 .7% over concatenation at the input, both on average and on all eight datasets, whereas encoding the plan over time changes the average MAE by at most 

2.2% . Second, prediction error and mechanism consistency diverge: the configuration with the lowest error is at or below chance in consistency on four of the five datasets with declared mechanisms, and no design choice avoids this. Finally, directional supervision, a loss term that penalizes the wrong-signed part of the response to a shifted action, raises consistency significantly on penalized mechanisms with no change in MAE. Together they give TSWMs a recipe: a frozen latent space and output-side fusion for accuracy, and a training objective for mechanism consistency.

Benchmarking Time-Series World Models 

Figure 1: Two plans from the same history. We sweep three design choices and score mechanism consistency. Many operational and clinical decisions depend on how a multivariate time series will evolve under differ-ent actions and exogenous inputs. For example, in an intensive-care unit, vital signs such as arterial blood pressure are held in an acceptable range by actions such as raising or lowering the infusion rate of a va-sopressor, and are moved as well by uncontrollable exogenous inputs. A model that takes the observed history and a plan of future actions and exogenous in-puts and predicts the response of the target is a world model in the sense used in reinforcement learning and video generation (Ha & Schmidhuber, 2018; Hafner et al., 2025; Agarwal et al., 2025); we call it a time series world model (TSWM). Current practice builds it as a forecasting model with actions and exogenous inputs as covariates (Das et al., 2023; Lim et al., 2021; Olivares et al., 2023; Arango et al., 2025; Wang et al., 2024), and

recent work has begun to call such models world models for time series (Ding et al., 2025; Liu et al., 2026; Qi et al., 2026). Each of these works proposes one design and compares it against few alternatives inside its own architecture, so the design choices of a TSWM have not been investigated systematically. By contrast, prior work on control problems and video generation has examined world models along two such choices: the space in which the state is predicted, observational (Agarwal et al., 2025) or a pretrained feature space (Assran et al., 2025; Zhou et al., 2024), and the way the action enters the model, by concatenation (Hafner et al., 2019; Zhou et al., 2024), as interleaved tokens (Assran et al., 2025), or as an additive embedding (Bruce et al., 2024). Neither has been tested on time series with real actions. In applications from multiple domains including retail demand, building control, and clinical prediction, models trained for prediction accuracy alone have been found to respond incorrectly to a change of action (Crasson et al., 2024; Kunz et al., 2023; Di Natale et al., 2022; Keogh & Van Geloven, 2024). A TSWM therefore needs a second property besides prediction accuracy, which we call mechanism consistency. A mechanism is a relation between an action and a state variable whose direction is known, and a mechanism-consistent model responds to a change of that action in that direction: a higher vasopressor infusion rate raises blood pressure, so raising the rate in the input should raise the predicted pressure. This property lets a world model answer what would happen under a plan that has not been taken, so a mechanism-consistent TSWM can compare candidate plans before one is carried out. However, existing benchmarks do not measure this. Forecasting benchmarks rarely contain action-conditional prediction (Godahewa et al., 2021; Aksu et al., 2024), and the benchmarks that score the reaction to an intervention usually test models on the dynamics of a simulator rather than of a real system (Hafner et al., 2019; Mu et al., 2026; Thumm et al., 2026). Two desiderata are therefore unmet: a benchmark on time series with real actions that compares the design choices of a TSWM systematically, and a formalization of the TSWM with a metric of mechanism consistency, so that the comparison evaluates the reaction to a change of action next to prediction error. We formalize the TSWM problem and build a benchmark for it. The formalization assigns each channel a role, the target as the state, what the controller sets as the actions, and everything else that moves the state as an exogenous input; distinguishes three forms of action, a continuous level such as a dose, a discrete mode such as heating or cooling, and a one-off event such as a meal; and adds mechanism consistency as a metric next to prediction error (Section 3.1). For dataset construction, we select eight public datasets with real actions, four from engineered infras-tructure, namely horticulture, building climate, district heating, and water treatment, and four from clinical care, namely anesthesia, two glucose-monitoring cohorts, and intensive care. Together they cover 12 ,205 systems, sampling intervals from 2 seconds to 30 minutes, and 41 action channels of all three forms (Section 4.1). On these datasets, the benchmark sweeps the three design components of a TSWM one at a time with the other two fixed: the prediction space, the observation space or the latent space of a frozen autoencoder (AE), a frozen variational autoencoder (VAE) or a joint-embedding predictive architecture (JEPA) (Assran et al., 2023) trained with the forecaster; the plan fusion, at the backbone input by concatenation or at its output by adapters such as FiLM-0 (Perez et al., 2018) and Gate (Dauphin et al., 2017); and the plan encoding over time, with or without a memory of the preceding steps. Each option is run with seven forecasting backbones and five seeds (Section 4). Our contributions are as follows. • We formalize the TSWM problem and build a benchmark for it from eight public datasets with real actions. Sweeping the three design choices shows that the frozen AE latent space is the best prediction space while JEPA is worse than the observation space, that FiLM-0 and Gate at the output are the best fusion methods and beat fusion at the input, and that a memory of the preceding steps in the plan encoding gives no consistent gain. • We find a contradiction between prediction accuracy and mechanism consistency: the configuration selected for prediction accuracy is at or below chance in mechanism consistency on four of the five datasets with declared mechanisms, and no design choice escapes this. • We propose directional supervision, a soft penalty on the sign of the response to a changed action, added to the training loss without changing the network, which raises mechanism consistency above 

0.99 on engineered and clinical mechanisms alike without loss of prediction accuracy (Section 5).

2 Related work 

Forecasting with exogenous inputs and actions. Covariate-aware forecasters differ in where the covariates enter the model and how they are combined with the target. Concatenation feeds them to the backbone input (Das et al., 2023); gating learns how much of them to pass (Lim et al., 2021; Han et al., 2026; Qiu et al., 2025b); addition keeps them in a separate path summed with the target (Olivares et al., 2023; Arango et al., 2025; Zhou et al., 2025); modulation scales the target representation instead (Qin et al., 2025); and cross-attention lets target tokens query covariate tokens (Wang et al., 2024; Yamaguchi et al., 2026), as it does for treatment sequences (Melnychuk et al., 2022; Bica et al., 2020). Foundation models that take covariates natively (Ansari et al., 2025; Hoo et al., 2025) predict in observation space and are outside our scope. How the effect of an input is spread over time is usually fixed by convention in each field: a carryover kernel in marketing (Jin et al., 2017), a learned decay in clinical series (Che et al., 2018). Each paper compares its design against two or three alternatives inside its own architecture, and none holds the backbone fixed while comparing the families. We hold the backbone fixed and split the plan path into a plan encoder and a fusion adapter that can be swapped independently. 

World models in other domains. A world model learns the state transition kernel p(xt+1 | x≤t, a ≤t)

and is judged by rolling it forward. RL rolls it out in a latent space (Ha & Schmidhuber, 2018; Hafner et al., 2019; 2025), vision makes it a controllable video generator (Yang et al., 2023; Agarwal et al., 2025) or a predictor in a pretrained feature space (Assran et al., 2025; Zhou et al., 2024), and medicine has begun to use the name for treatment-conditioned disease trajectories (Ding et al., 2025; Liu et al., 2026). Two choices recur: whether the encoder is shaped by the dynamics, and whether the action enters by concatenation, interleaved tokens, or an additive embedding. Time series forecasting has only recently moved into a learned latent space (Yang et al., 2026), and its benchmarks do not score the response to a change of action. We study both choices on time series with real actions and add that score next to the forecast error. 

Constraining learned dynamics with prior knowledge. Prior knowledge enters a learned dynamics model as the residual of a governing equation in the loss (Raissi et al., 2019; Karniadakis et al., 2021), as a monotonicity constraint built into the architecture (Nolte et al., 2023; Runje & Shankaranarayana, 2023), or as a loss on the ranking of treatment effects in a hybrid mechanistic–neural model (Zou et al., 2024). The treatment-effect models above instead recover the effect from observational data by correcting for confounding, and Crasson et al. (2024) show that forecast accuracy and causal accuracy can come apart. We impose the sign instead of learning it, because our actions come from a controller that reacts to the state, and the observational sign is often the reverse of the mechanism. Our penalty needs only the sign of a relation; it is a term in the loss, leaves the network unchanged, and is checked by a finite-difference intervention on the same held-out windows, reported alongside the forecast error.

4 Benchmarking the Design Choices 

4.1 Datasets 

Table 1 summarizes the eight public datasets. We selected them because they record actions as defined in Section 3.1. The four engineered datasets come from horticulture, building HVAC, district heating and wastewater treatment; the four clinical datasets come from anesthesia, two continuous-glucose-monitoring studies and critical care. The engineered datasets have exogenous inputs, and three of them also have mode actions; the clinical datasets have neither, but they are the only datasets with event actions. In total there are 31 continuous action channels, 7 mode channels and 3 event channels covering 

37 event types, so each of the three action components is present in at least two datasets. Sampling intervals range from 2 s to 30 min, the number of systems from 1 to 8,478 , and the state dimension is at most 6 in every dataset. Each channel is min–max scaled to the range given in Section 3.1. The scaling bounds are fixed per dataset and shared by all methods; they are taken from instrument or physiological limits where such limits exist and from percentiles of the data otherwise. Mode and event channels are represented as integer indices into an embedding table, one index per condition in Mj or per set in V, plus one reserved index for missing codes and for sets not seen in training. Appendix A gives the channel roles, scaling ranges and split of each dataset. Computing mechanism consistency requires a set of declared mechanisms. Each mechanism specifies a continuous action channel, a state channel that it affects, and the sign of the effect. Table 2 lists the twenty-one mechanisms used in this paper. All of them were fixed before any model was trained or evaluated; in this section they are used only for evaluation, and in Section 5 a subset of them enters the training objective. For the engineered datasets, the signs are taken from the documented operation of each system: for example, a hotter heating pipe raises the greenhouse air temperature, opening a vent lowers it, opening the control valve increases flow and heat power, and the controlled temperature follows its setpoint. For the clinical datasets, the signs are taken from the pharmacology of each drug: propofol deepens hypnosis and lowers blood pressure, remifentanil lowers heart rate and blood pressure, vasopressors raise blood pressure, inotropes raise heart rate, and a higher inspired oxygen fraction raises oxygen saturation. The two PleiaData mechanisms depend on the operating mode: raising the setpoint increases the indoor temperature in heating mode and decreases it in cooling mode. Only five of the eight datasets are used for this metric. 4.2 Experimental Setup 

We run three experiments, each of which varies one of the design choices in Section 3.2 while fixing the other two. Experiment 1 compares the prediction spaces with Concat fusion and Instant plan encoding. Experiment 2 compares the fusion methods with the AE prediction space, which Experiment 1 selects, and Instant plan encoding. Experiment 3 compares the plan encodings with the AE prediction space and with each of Gate and FiLM-0, the two best fusion methods in Experiment 2. Every configuration is trained with seven backbones, TimeXer (Wang et al., 2024), TiDE (Das et al., 2023), DUET (Qiu et al., 2025a), PatchTST (Nie et al., 2022), TimeKAN (Huang et al., 2025), CrossLinear (Zhou et al., 2025) and Amplifier (Fei et al., 2025), on all eight datasets with five random seeds, under a single training protocol (Table 6) and the fixed data split of Appendix A. For each run we keep the checkpoint with the lowest validation loss and report its validation MAE on the scaled state channels as the prediction error. The same checkpoints are then evaluated for mechanism consistency, without any retraining, using the MC metric of Section 3.1 with |δ| = 0 .1 in both directions on the twenty-one mechanisms in Table 2. Appendices B to H give the formulas and implementation of all options, hyperparameters, statistical tests, extended results, an auxiliary-loss and rollout diagnostic, and the complete result grids.

4.3 Results Prediction space. The top block of Table 3 compares the four prediction spaces, pooling the same backbones and seeds for each dataset. The two frozen latent spaces perform best. AE has the lowest mean rank and beats observation-space prediction on all eight datasets. VAE is close to AE, within 3% 

on average (Appendix D). The jointly trained JEPA is worse than observation-space prediction. We therefore fix the prediction space to AE in the remaining experiments. A likely reason is the frozen, pretrained latent space itself: JEPA also predicts in a latent space, but its encoder is trained jointly, and it is worse than observation space. 

Plan fusion. The middle block of Table 3 compares the seven fusion methods under the same AE representation. The two modulation methods work best: Gate and FiLM-0 have the best mean ranks, both beat Concat and None on all eight datasets, and they differ by less than 1% on average. The residual methods also do well, and cross-attention has no consistent advantage over these simpler adapters. Adjusting the predicted representation with the plan is therefore an effective way to bring in action and exogenous information. We keep both Gate and FiLM-0 for the encoding experiment; detailed comparisons are in Appendix D. 

Plan encoding. The bottom block of Table 3 tests whether letting the encoding of each step depend on the preceding steps improves prediction. Conv has the best mean rank and a small average improvement over Instant, 2.2% , but the gain holds on only four of the eight datasets (Appendix D). Decay and Attn give no consistent improvement either. A memory of the preceding steps can therefore help on individual datasets, but we find no general benefit beyond the history summaries that the fusion module already receives.

5 Learning Mechanism-Consistent Responses 

Section 4.3 shows that the forecasting objective supervises fθ(h, a ) only under the plan that was carried out and provides no target under an alternative plan. We add this supervision with a method we call directional supervision. Given the same history, we compare forecasts under the executed and the perturbed plan and penalize the part of their difference that opposes a declared mechanism. We apply this objective to the benchmark’s AE–Gate–Instant configuration.

5.2 Results 

We train on the six datasets with continuous actions, with the backbones, seeds, split and protocol of Section 4.2, and pair each run with the Gate run of Experiment 2 that shares its dataset, backbone and seed. The penalty covers twelve mechanisms of Table 2, three on Greenhouse, one on PleiaData, two on PREDIST, two on VitalDB and four on MIMIC-Cardio; the remaining nine are held out, and Wastewater, which has no penalized mechanism, serves as a null control. Figure 4 reports MC at 

δ = +0 .1 on held-out windows and validation MAE, with the paired change per run; Appendix F gives every number. MC on the penalized mechanisms rises on every dataset to a mean above 0.99 over the 

35 runs on each mechanism, including the windward vent, on which Gate scores 0.196 , and four of the six clinical mechanisms, on which Gate is below chance. The downward shift, which the penalty never sees, is as consistent as the upward one, so the learned response holds on held-out windows and in the untrained direction. Prediction error is unchanged: the median change in validation MAE, pooled over datasets, is −0.05% , and the per-dataset medians lie between −1.54% and +0 .35% , inside the ±5% band; MIMIC-Cardio shows a small improvement, and PleiaData has the widest spread across runs. The gain is confined to the supplied signs: the held-out drug mechanisms stay below chance except dopamine, and three held-out mechanisms move away from their sign, the PleiaData cooling-mode setpoint, vasopressin and remifentanil on mean pressure (Appendix F). Directional supervision therefore enforces the signs it is given and only those: it does not recover undeclared mechanisms and does not constrain effect sizes. Within that scope, it gives a forecaster that keeps the accuracy of the best adapter and responds to a changed plan in the declared direction, which no choice in the design space achieved.

6 Conclusion 

We formalized the time series world model, a forecaster that takes the observed history and a plan of actions and exogenous inputs and predicts the response of the state, and asked two questions that current practice leaves open: which design choices matter, and whether an accurate forecaster responds to a changed plan the way the real system does. A benchmark of eight public datasets with real actions, swept one design choice at a time, answers the first: a frozen latent prediction space and gated fusion at the output lower the error across datasets and backbones, whereas a memory in the plan encoding brings no consistent gain. Mechanism consistency, a metric that scores the direction of the response to a shifted action, answers the second in the negative: the most accurate configurations are at or below chance on most datasets, and no design choice escapes this, because the sign in the training data is observational and an architecture does not change the data. Directional supervision supplies the sign through the loss and raises consistency to near its maximum at no cost in error, so a mechanism-consistent world model is a property of the training objective rather than of the architecture. Future work includes extending the supervision to event actions, clinical validation of the declared pharmacological signs, and evaluation under closed-loop control.

This work studies action-conditioned forecasting on existing public datasets and pretrained models; no new data were collected from human subjects. A.2 Scaling ranges 

The scaling range of a channel is fixed once per dataset before training. Greenhouse, PleiaData, Vi-talDB, CGMacros, Shanghai and the ventilator channels of MIMIC-Cardio use constants declared from instrument or physiological limits, e.g. 40 to 400 mg/dL for glucose. Wastewater uses the 1st and 99th percentiles of the training segment, PREDIST uses the 1st and 99th percentiles of each substation’s own series, and the drug channels of MIMIC-Cardio use the 99.5th percentile of the non-zero doses over the cohort. The continuous actions of Greenhouse, VitalDB and MIMIC-Cardio have declared ranges whose lower end is an off position and map to [0 , 1] . All other channels map to [−1, 1] , including the PREDIST valve position and the Wastewater dosing rates, whose ranges are percentiles of the data, so their zero, although it is an off position, is not mapped to a fixed point. 

A.3 Splits and windows 

Seven datasets contain several systems of one kind, and we split them by system: all windows of one system go either to the training set or to the held-out set, so that a model is tested on systems it has never seen and the N training windows, which overlap in time within a system, are not independent samples. The system is the team for Greenhouse, the sensor–HVAC pair for PleiaData, the substation for PREDIST, the surgical case for VitalDB, the subject for CGMacros and Shanghai, and the patient for MIMIC-Cardio; the train/held-out counts are in Table 1. Wastewater covers a single plant and is split in time, the first 80% for training and the rest held out. Within a system, the series is cut wherever two consecutive samples are further apart than a tolerance, and every window lies entirely inside one of the resulting segments. The tolerance is 30 minutes for Greenhouse, PleiaData, PREDIST, CGMacros and Shanghai, 10 minutes for Wastewater and 2 minutes for VitalDB; a MIMIC-Cardio stay is a single contiguous segment on its 30-minute grid, so it is never cut.

E.1 Declared mechanisms and exclusions 

The six datasets with a continuous action declare twenty-four relations, read from the documented process for the engineered datasets and from the pharmacology of the drug for the clinical ones. Two are set aside before any model is evaluated. The greenhouse assimilation lamp runs at full output for 

98 .8% of the held-out steps, so the upward quantile shift τi,δ changes the plan in almost no window and the conditioning event of MC nearly never holds. The PleiaData on/off relation is categorical, and τi,δ is defined for continuous channels only; Appendix F probes it by switching the unit on and reports it separately. The remaining twenty-two are listed in Table 11. One of them, the single relation of Wastewater, is set aside after evaluation and is reported in the table but not counted. It states that metal-salt dosing lowers the phosphate concentration in the tank by precipitation, a mechanism that acts through the reaction and the residence of the water in the tank, on a timescale beyond the 32 -minute horizon ( H = 16 steps at 2 min) over which MC averages the response, so the declared sign is not expected to appear within the window the metric scores. Its scores, 0.03 to 0.21 across the design choices, are the lowest in the grid; we report them so that this exclusion, made after the numbers were seen, stays visible. Section 4.3 therefore averages the twenty-one relations of the remaining five datasets. 

E.2 Counted windows and reproducibility check 

MC conditions on the perturbation changing the plan and on the forecast moving. Both conditions bite. A channel already at its maximum cannot be shifted up, and a model that routes the plan into a path with no influence on the output produces |∆i→cθ | below the threshold in every window; those cells have no defined consistency and are reported as such rather than counted as chance. None is the extreme case, since it never receives the plan, but it is not the only one: 85 of the 3150 cells that do receive the plan also fail to move, and every one of them is TiDE under a prediction space that routes the plan through the backbone rather than an adapter. Every number of Section 4.3 comes from the checkpoints of the three experiments; no model is retrained. Reloading a checkpoint and recomputing its validation MAE reproduces the stored value to 3.6 × 10 −6

for every backbone but DUET, whose mixture-of-experts router samples at inference and drifts by at most 1.1 × 10 −3. Within one probed cell the router is held to a single sample across the factual and the perturbed forecast, which is what a counterfactual requires: the same unit, the same history, and the same draw of any internal randomness, with only the plan changed. The factual forecast is verified to be bitwise identical when the plan is passed twice.

F Directional Supervision: Details and Extended Results 

This appendix supports Section 5. It gives the hyperparameters of the added term and how ρ and the penalized set were fixed, the score of every mechanism under both shift directions, and the prediction error of every dataset including the null control. 

F.1 Hyperparameters and the penalized set 

Directional supervision adds the term of Equation 5.2 to the forecasting protocol of Table 6 and changes nothing else: the same AE–Gate–Instant model, backbones, seeds, split, windows, optimizer and stopping rule; each mechanism costs one extra pass through Gθ and D per batch. The term uses ρ = 0 .1,one shift size per batch drawn from δ ∼ U (0 .05 , 0.30) and applied upward only, ε = 10 −6, and the quantiles of τi,δ estimated on the training actions; it is excluded from early stopping, which uses the forecasting loss alone. The value of ρ follows a rule fixed before the grid was run: the smallest value in 

{0.1, 1, 10 } whose consistency on the penalized mechanisms of the training windows reached 0.95 in a Greenhouse pilot with three backbones. All three values reached 1.000 , so the rule selects ρ = 0 .1.The penalized set holds twelve of the twenty-one mechanisms of Table 2 and was fixed before any supervised model was trained. Mechanisms were assigned by action channel, so that the two targets of the PREDIST valve and the two targets of norepinephrine are on the same side and no held-out mechanism shares its actuator with a penalized one; the single exception is the PleiaData cooling-mode setpoint, which shares its channel with the penalized heating-mode mechanism and differs only in the mode condition, so it tests whether the learned response follows the mode. On the clinical datasets the held-out side was chosen to contain both a mechanism that Gate already gets right, dopamine, and mechanisms it gets wrong, phenylephrine and remifentanil. Wastewater has no penalized mechanism: its single relation is set aside in Appendix E.1, so the added term is identically zero there and the dataset serves as a null control. The PleiaData on/off relation is listed with the penalized set in the code but the term is defined for continuous channels only and is zero for it, so Figure 4 leaves it out of the PleiaData mean. It is probed by switching the unit from off to on instead of by the quantile shift, and Table 12 reports it with Wastewater in a separate block.

G Auxiliary Prediction Loss and Rollout Diagnostic 

This appendix reports the diagnostic that Section 4.2 mentions. As a separate diagnostic, we fix AE, Gate, and Instant and add supervision to the pre-fusion state forecast: 

Lprediction = MSE( by, y ) + λ MSE  D(zpre ), y , λ ∈ { 0, 0.1, 0.3}. (G.1) Here zpre is the backbone forecast before fusion, D is the frozen decoder, and by is the full model’s output. Both terms are in observation space; backbone-specific training regularizers are retained. This is an additional objective comparison, not a fourth architectural sweep. Equation G.1 adds observation-space supervision to the decoded pre-fusion forecast. The validation criterion used for checkpoint selection excludes this added term, retaining the full-model prediction criterion. The bypass metric decodes the pre-fusion forecast alone for a single 16 -step block. It is not a forecast of a no-action intervention: its training target is still the observed outcome under the recorded plan.
