# A Design Concept of Forecasting Software for Normalized Vector Autoregressions with Fat Tails and Stochastic Volatility 

Fei Shang a, Xiaolei Wang b, Tomasz Woźniak b

> aSchool of Economics and Trade, Guangdong University of Foreign Studies, Guangzhou, 510006, China
> bDepartment of Economics, University of Melbourne, 111 Barry Street, Carlton, 3053, VIC, Australia

Abstract 

We present a suite of R packages for macroeconomic forecasting that leverages advanced Bayesian, structural, multivariate, dynamic, hierarchical, non-linear, and non-Gaussian models. The suite enables both structural and predictive analyses, and is adapted to time series data across various types, dimensions, and sampling frequencies. Each additional feature increases computational complexity. To address this challenge, our software design incorporates a carefully curated selection of models, efficient algorithms implemented in 

C++ , advanced econometric and numerical methods, robust handling of complex input and output objects, and standardised workflows. This approach combines the computational efficiency of C++ with the convenience of working with data in R. We demonstrate that our packages facilitate original research contributions in forecasting, as illustrated by our example in which vector autoregressions with non-centred stochastic volatility enhance density and point predictions relative to models with centred stochastic volatility. 

Keywords: Post-COVID Forecasting, Non-Centred Stochastic Volatility, Order-Invariant, Structural Models, Student-t Errors, R packages, bsvars , bsvarSIGNs , bvars 

1arXiv:2608.28087v1 [econ.EM] 28 Aug 2026 1. Introduction 

Macroeconomic forecasting is crucial for policy-makers, businesses, and researchers to make informed decisions in an ever-changing economic landscape. These decisions affect crucial economic aggregates and other indicators determining well-being at all future horizons (see Rostami-Tabar et al., 2022). The gravity of such prediction-driven economic planing, institutional design requiring interpretability, and complex properties of macroeconomic and financial time series lead to a burgeoning field of enquiry focusing on scalable dynamic system modelling (see Doan et al., 1984). Additionally, the global financial crisis and the COVID-19 pandemic highlighted the importance of robust forecasting models that can adapt to unprecedented shocks and volatility in economic data (see Lenza and Primiceri, 2022). Our forecasting software design accommodates these requirements ensuring transparency and reproducibility of predictive analyses. However, its practical usability faces even more challenges arising from the numerical complexity of handling Bayesian hierarchical and non-linear modelling. Moreover, point and density forecasting handles forecasting of measurements and latent dynamic processes jointly, resulting in high dimensionality of numerical integration. In this context, we present a suite of R packages designed for forecasting using Vector Autoregressions (VARs) with Stochastic Volatility (SV) and Student-t errors, which are particularly suited for handling complex non-linear dynamics in time series data. The suite includes three packages: bsvars by Woźniak (2025a,b) implementing the heteroskedastic structural VARs by Lütkepohl et al. (2026) and Shang and Woźniak (2026), package 

bsvarSIGNs by Wang and Woźniak (2025a,b) focusing on the hierarchical VARs for post-COVID forecasting by Giannone et al. (2015) and Lenza and Primiceri (2022), and 2package bvars by Liu et al. (2026) providing the flexible VAR model by Chan (2020). The focus is on normalised models with well-specified covariance matrix of the predictive density, facilitating scalable robust predictions. In the current paper, we highlight the bespoke design of the three open-source packages. Together, they address the challenges through the following features: • deliberate selection of modelling approaches representing commonly used specifications, • a range of forecasting techniques accommodating best practices in the field, • fast computations by relying on algorithms written in C++ ,• supported by adaptation of frontier econometric and numerical methods, • handling multi-layered inputs and outputs by object-oriented programming, and • coherent forecasting workflows enabling customisation and transparency. Taken together these packages offer an unmatched versatility in dealing with data and offering ample opportunities for generating original predictive analyses. We illustrate these opportunities by an example of forecasting a 10-variable monetary-fiscal system to document the merit of presented approaches. In particular, the VARs with non-centred SV proposed by Lütkepohl et al. (2026) and in the current manuscript perform best in terms of point and density forecasting 1-quarter and 1-year ahead. 

2. A Suite of Predictive Models 

The packages include a selection of state-of-the-art models that enable both structural and predictive analyses across a wide range of specifications. This suite of models is created in 3accordance with recent literature and established best practices. Specifically, it features non-normal, heteroskedastic, structural and reduced-form VARs with hierarchical priors. These characteristics are incorporated based on substantial evidence supporting their effectiveness in improving forecasting performance, including that provided by Doan et al. (1984) and Giannone et al. (2015) for the Minnesota prior and hierarchical prior shrinkage, by Clark (2011) for conditional heteroskedasticity, by Chiu et al. (2017) and Chan (2020) for non-normal errors, and by Chan et al. (2024) for order-invariance. Also normalisation of predictive density covariance enhances the models’ capacity to handle diverse datasets and increases the numerical stability of estimation and forecasting. The packages provide both general models and nested specifications, as detailed in Woźniak (2025b) and Wang and Woźniak (2025a). 3. Forecasting Software Features 

Our packages implement Bayesian, structural, multivariate, dynamic, hierarchical, non-linear, and non-normal models that are specifically designed for robust forecasting, adaptability, and scalability. However, each listed feature comes at the cost of increased computational complexity. Our design addresses this challenge by relying on algorithms written in C++ , implementing frontier numerical methods, handling complex input and output objects, and enabling adjustable forecasting workflows. Therefore, we combine the best of both approaches: fast algorithms written in C++ and the convenience of data analysis in R, which has a decisive role in making these methods available to practitioners. 

3.1. Reliance on Algorithms Written in C++ 

In our packages, routines for estimation, forecasting, and processing of the rich estimation output, such as computing forecast error variance decomposition, are implemented using 

C++ code. This task is facilitated by the Rcpp package by Eddelbuettel et al. (2011), which automates compilation and linking of the C++ code and ensures object compatibility with R. We rely heavily on linear algebra and pseudo-random number generators, which 8are facilitated using the package RcppArmadillo by Eddelbuettel and Sanderson (2014), a collection of headers linking to the C++ library armadillo by Sanderson and Curtin (2016). Both algebraic operations and random number generation are performed much faster with RcppArmadillo than using many alternative solutions we tested. For instance, obtaining 1000 random draws from a standard normal distribution using RcppArmadillo ,

arma::vec out(1000, arma::fill::randn); , is 33 per cent faster on average than 

Rcpp implementation, Rcpp::NumericVector out = Rcpp::rnorm(1000, 0, 1); , with even larger differential for other solutions. We also use C++ functions for linear algebra on tri-diagonal matrices from package stochvol by Hosszejni and Kastner (2021), and specialised samplers from truncated normal and generalised inverse Gaussian distributions using packages RcppTN by Olmsted (2017) and GIGrvg by Leydold and Hörmann (2023), respectively. All of these developments make the algorithms computationally fast. Still, estimation of our models is demanding and may take a while, which we address by providing a progress bar implemented with the RcppProgress package by Forner (2020). As an additional feature contributing to open source forecasting community, our packages export both: essential R functions and all C++ functions using the Rcpp 

attribute, // [[Rcpp::interfaces(cpp)]] (see Allaire et al., 2026), and providing dynamic libraries bsvars , bsvarSIGNs , and bvars . Therefore, developers of R packages can use our functions directly in their C++ code bypassing the imports of R functions. 

3.2. Numerical Methods 

A number of frontier numerical methods tailored to the particular model specifications were implemented in our Bayesian estimation procedures, thereby reducing computational complexity. For instance, our empirical example applies VARs to 10 quarterly variables, 9requiring an intercept and 4 lags and resulting in 410 autoregressive parameters. Sampling these parameters efficiently is crucial for the overall performance of the estimation procedure. In reduced-form models from packages bsvarSIGNs and bvars these parameters are sampled from a matrix-variate normal full conditional posterior distribution, the covariance matrix of which is defined by the Kronecker product of two smaller matrices, greatly reducing the burden of inverting this matrix (see Chan, 2020). Further computational gains are possible in the structural models in package bsvars ,where these parameters are sampled equation-by-equation as in Carriero et al. (2022). Similarly, bsvars implements the row-by-row Gibbs sampler for the structural matrix proposed by Waggoner and Zha (2003), exhibiting efficient mixing and fast convergence. Our 

C++ implementation of this sampler was over 60 times faster than equivalent R code we had optimised over years, for an empirically relevant structural matrix of dimensions 8 × 8.Furthermore, the models in package bsvarSIGNs apply a feasible decomposition of the joint posterior distribution into a marginal density for hyper-parameters and a conditional one for autoregressive parameters and the covariance matrix, as in Giannone et al. (2015). This decomposition results in a Metropolis-Hastings algorithm for a low-dimensional distribution over hyper-parameters and an independent sampler for the remaining parameters parallelised using function mclapply and parLapply from package parallel .Optimal approaches to estimating parameters also drive the allocation of models into the packages. The guiding principle is to gather models implying similar numerical procedures. The package bsvars includes structural models that rely on row-by-row estimation of the parameters, whereas bvars includes reduced-form models. In these two packages, estimation is performed using Gibbs sampler implying serial computations. The bsvarSIGNs package focuses on models relying on independent sampler facilitating parallelisation. 10 Estimation of conditional heteroskedasticity poses further challenges, as the dimension of the latent processes driving volatility equals to the time dimension. Both packages 

bsvars and bvars apply the same techniques for the estimation of SV. They rely on the transformation of the non-linear non-normal state-space model into a much faster-to-estimate linear, conditionally Gaussian representation proposed by Omori et al. (2007), where the conditional normality is achieved by approximating the log-χ21

distribution by a mixture of 10 normal components with fixed parameters. Our common SV models rely on similar normal mixture approximation of the log-χ2 

> N

distribution, where 

N is the number of variables, estimated using a fast C++ implementation by Sanderson and Curtin (2017). This approach to SV estimation requires sampling from a multivariate normal distribution of dimension equal to the sample size, which is computationally expensive. We take advantage of the fact that the covariance matrix of this distribution is specified by a banded precision matrix as in Chan and Jeliazkov (2009). An appropriate implementation of the code from the package stochvol facilitates over 10-fold computational gains relative to less efficient implementations as documented by Woźniak (2021). Finally, to grant fast convergence of SV estimation, allowing users to reduce the length of the Markov Chain Monte Carlo (MCMC), we implement the ancillarity-sufficiency interweaving strategy by Kastner and Frühwirth-Schnatter (2014). 

3.3. Handling Inputs and Outputs 

Providing software packages for Bayesian forecasting requires handling complicated input and output objects. We code these structures by applying object-oriented programming using the R6 package by Chang (2021) to combine the simplicity of initiating the forecasting workflows with ample possibilities for customisation. Consider an example in which the SVAR-SV model from package bsvars is applied to fiscal policy as in Lütkepohl et al. 11 (2026). Begin by investigating the model specification that includes fixed hyper-parameters of prior distributions, starting values for all estimated parameters, processed data matrices, identification restrictions for structural models, and some parameters determining particular model features. Apply the R6 initialisation by executing function specify_bsvar_sv , using the package-provided data matrix us_fiscal_sww , for a model with p = 4 lags, as follows: 

spec = specify_bsvar_sv$new(us_fiscal_sww, p = 4) 

The created object spec is of class BSVARSV that includes elements specifying our models that can be accessed as list elements. Therefore, investigating data matrices can be done by executing spec$data_matrices , which itself is an R6 object of class 

DataMatricesBSVAR with elements spec$data_matrices$Y and spec$data_matrices$X .Similarly, the fixed prior hyper-parameters are collected in element spec$prior of class 

PriorBSVARSV . For instance, the prior mean of the autoregressive parameters can be accessed via spec$prior$A . A careful modification of this element and other elements of 

spec$prior preserving their type and dimensions will adjust the prior distributions for the empirical analysis. Finally, the model identification and starting values can be accessed and modified via element spec$identification of class IdentificationBSVARs and 

spec$starting_values of class StartingValuesBSVARSV , respectively. The separation of the model specification and estimation is a distinguishing feature of our packages that aims at achieving transparency, flexibility, and possibilities for customisation of the forecasting workflows. Therefore, the model specification can be customised by either providing arguments to the initialisation function, with help available via ?specify_bsvar_sv , or by modifying the elements of the created object spec . The 12 specification object also includes utility functions that are used internally by the package to manage the workflows, such as spec$get_normal() or spec$get_identification() .

3.4. Forecasting Workflows 

The empirical analysis following the model specification requires more detailed explanation of the workflow. It is organised around verbs estimate , forecast , compute , and verify ,and complemented by the plot and summary functions. These verbs define the generics as well as the methods for the R6 classes, resulting in uniform workflows for all our packages. Focusing on the forecasting workflow, consider the first two verbs. estimate is a generic function defined in package bsvars with the methods all across our packages specific to the implemented models. On the other hand, forecast is a generic imported from package 

generics by Wickham et al. (2025) with the methods defined for each of our estimation output classes. This solution allows simultaneous work with other forecasting packages that implement the same generic function. We continue the example and estimate the SVAR-SV model. The package design leads to a two-stage process illustrated by the following code. 

burn = estimate(spec, S = 10000) post = estimate(burn, S = 10000) 

In the first stage the object spec is provided to function estimate() with the argument S= 10000 to obtain 10,000 draws from the posterior distribution via Gibbs sampler. This step is run to achieve convergence. In fact, this line executes method estimate.BSVARSV 

for the class BSVARSV and returns an object burn of class PosteriorBSVARSV that includes two elements, namely burn$posterior with the draws from the posterior distribution, and 

burn$last_draw of class BSVARSV whose element burn$last_draw$starting_values 

13 contains now the last draw of the MCMC. In the second stage, the object burn is passed to the method estimate.PosteriorBSVARSV , that initiates the Gibbs sampler at 

burn$last_draw$starting_values , and continues the MCMC to obtain the final 10,000 draws from the stationary posterior distribution. The output is saved in object post of class PosteriorBSVARSV with the same structure as already described. This object is subsequently used for structural analyses and forecasting. The object post containing the estimation output is passed to function forecast() 

and complemented by the specification of the forecast horizon in argument horizon . The following code calls the method forecast.PosteriorBSVARSV applying the sampler from the predictive density of the particular model, returns an object of class Forecasts saved in 

fore , and plots predictions using the method plot.Forecasts defined in package bsvars .

fore = forecast(post, horizon = 8) plot(fore) 

The R6 object of class Forecasts lists the draws from the predictive density in element fore$forecasts that can be used to compute any quantity reporting the forecast, including the mean, standard deviation, and the 5th and 95th percentiles provided by executing summary(fore) . The object fore also contains the mean and covariance of the conditional predictive densities the draws were generated from in elements 

fore$forecast_mean and fore$forecast_covariance , respectively. They can be used to compute measures of density forecasting performance, such as the log predictive score by Geweke and Amisano (2010). Finally, the same workflow can be implemented using the pipe operator |> . As the computational burden is in estimation, we propose to isolate it in the following code: 14 us_fiscal_sww |> specify_bsvar_sv$new(p = 4) |> estimate(S = 10000) |> estimate(S = 10000) -> post 

from other stages of analysis, such as the forecasting presented in the following line: 

post |> forecast(horizon = 8) |> plot() 

All of these methods are implemented for a wide range of models collected in our packages granting users access to a variety of forecasting approaches. 

4. Post-COVID Forecasting of a Monetary-Fiscal System 

The post-COVID forecasting performance of the models from our developed packages is evaluated using an expanding-window recursive forecasting exercise for the 10-variable quarterly system proposed by Mountford and Uhlig (2009). The dataset comprises total tax revenue, government spending, gross domestic product (GDP), the federal funds rate, consumption, real wages, investment, M2 money stock, the producer price index of crude materials, and the GDP deflator, covering the period from Q1 1959 to Q3 2025. The initial forecast origin is Q4 2020. Forecasting performance is assessed at 1-quarter ( h = 1 ) and 1-year ( h = 4 ) horizons using the predictive log-score (PLS) by Geweke and Amisano (2010) and the mean absolute scaled error (MASE) by Hyndman and Koehler (2006). Our model performance assessment focuses on comparing models with centred SV exhibiting excellent performance according to Clark (2011), Carriero et al. (2016), Chan (2020), and Chan et al. (2024), to those with non-centred SV proposed by Lütkepohl et al. (2026). Overall, the comparison includes 19 models, which differ according to the features 15 presented in Table 1. These features include volatility processes: non-centred SV (ncSV), centred SV (cSV), and the homoskedastic model (const)—as well as error term distributions, which are set to either normal (norm) or Student-t (t). Additionally, 11 structural models from the package bsvars differ in terms of order invariance, which is present in models with an unrestricted matrix B (OI) and absent in models with a lower-triangular B (LT). Forecasts are also generated using reduced-form models, including 6 from the package bvars and 2 from bsvarSIGNs . All eight reduced-form models are order invariant, as their covariance is estimated explicitly by the matrix Σ and they feature common volatility. These models differ along features similar to those of the structural models, except for model 18, which incorporates COVID-specific volatility by Lenza and Primiceri (2022). Many of these models were used in prominent studies listed in the note to Table 1. We summarise the forecasting performance by reporting average predictive scores, calculated as the mean exponent of the PLS for each feature at each forecast horizon. At both forecast horizons, non-centred SV models outperform centred SV models. In particular, non-centred SV raises the average exponentiated PLS by approximately 6% at 

h = 1 and 15% at h = 4 relative to centred SV, with similar tendencies holding when structural or reduced-form models are considered only. Analysing the average MASE, alternative SV parameterisations yield very similar point forecast accuracy, with relative differences below 1%. Additionally, according to both performance measures t-distributed models outperform normal models and structural models outperform reduced-form models at both horizons. 16 5. Conclusions 

We present a suite of open-source packages designed to provide a unified framework for Bayesian VAR forecasting. Their novel combination of features makes scalable and reproducible predictions possible using normalised heteroskedastic non-Gaussian models. The reported forecasting performance measures show that these features are essential to obtain precise forecasts, which emphasises the importance of our software design.
