# LineupRL: Verifiable Reinforcement Learning for Time Series Captioning via Caption-to-Series Identification 

Haochen Zhang 1, Laura Yao 1, Zachary Plotkin 2, Gengwei Zhang 1, Tianlong Chen 1,2 B

> 1

University of North Carolina at Chapel Hill 2Foci Labs Time series captioning is a fundamental step in time series understanding and can also serve as the bridge between signal and natural language. Supervised fine-tuning (SFT) relies on a larger model’s captions and cannot exceed their quality. Reinforcement learning (RL) can, but its rewards were designed for other modalities and other tasks, and they transfer poorly to open-ended generation in the time series domain. We address this by proposing LineupRL, a reinforcement learning with verifiable rewards (RLVR) pipeline whose reward is caption-to-series identification. The reward model is a frozen large language model (LLM) verifier that reads the generated caption and the candidate time series as raw values, never the chart, and must pick the described time series from multiple distractors. Matching is a far lighter demand on the verifier than writing questions or judging a caption, so an off-the-shelf LLM can supply the reward. Across two captioning benchmarks, and on forecasting and reconstruction where the predictor sees only the caption, LineupRL outperforms SFT and RL baselines on every metric. The 3B vision language model (VLM) trained by LineupRL also outperforms, at 1/24 of the parameters, the 72B VLM whose captions the SFT baseline is distilled from. Our case study shows that LineupRL resists reward hacking, and that the captioner it trains both traces the trend and names the values at key points.

Time series record how a system evolves over time, and decisions in healthcare, finance and climate depend on read-ing them accurately. Time series captioning automates this reading. Given a time series, a captioner writes a natural-language description of how the signal behaves (Jhamtani & Berg-Kirkpatrick, 2021; Sen et al., 2025; Zhou et al., 2026). Time series captions are useful from multiple perspectives. For human practitioners, text is easier to act on than the raw signal. For example, neonatal intensive-care staff chose bet-ter treatments from textual summaries of physiological time series than from the corresponding trend graphs (Law et al., 2005). For language-oriented systems, a caption provides a text interface to the signal, and this interface has been used for domain reporting (Fons et al., 2024b), forecast explana-tion (Aksu et al., 2024) and clinical prediction (Ji et al., 2026). The task dates back to rule-based data-to-text systems (Ku-kich, 1983; Reiter et al., 2005; Yu et al., 2007; Portet et al., 2009) and later data-driven models (Lloyd et al., 2014; Jandaghi & Pujara, 2020; Jhamtani & Berg-Kirkpatrick, 2021), both confined to fixed rules or output formats. Large language models (LLMs), vision-language models (VLMs) and time-series-language models (TSLMs) remove this restriction by open-ended generation, and recent work improves their captions by supervised fine-tuning (SFT)

on time series paired with captions. Because human-written captions exist only in small collections (Jhamtani & Berg-Kirkpatrick, 2021), these pairs are synthesized, either by prompting a larger model to describe the time series (Trabelsi et al., 2025; Lin et al., 2026) or by generating the text from attributes known by construction (Xie et al., 2024; Feng et al., 2026). However, SFT has two weaknesses in this setting. First, it is bounded by the supervised data. A captioner trained to imitate synthesized captions can be no better than the model that wrote them, and it inherits that model’s disadvantages, including plausible but incorrect statements about the time series (Trabelsi et al., 2025). Second, it generalizes poorly. Trained on a limited number of pairs, the captioner may memorize the reference captions rather than learning to read the time series, and what it memorized transfers poorly to time series it has not seen (Chu et al., 2025). On CaTS-Bench, open-source models fine-tuned on its synthetic pairs still trail proprietary models on numeric detail, and the fine-tuned model states statistics it cannot compute as confident guesses, which the benchmark attributes to a cross-entropy objective that treats number tokens like any other token (Zhou et al., 2026). In our own experiment, fine-tuning a 3B VLM on captions from a 72B teacher does not raise its entailment of the reference captions on BEDTime and lowers its series-to-caption identification on CaTS-Bench below the untuned initialization (Table 1). We call this failure mode ungrounded captioning. The caption is fluent and plausible, but it misses the important features of the time series, because the captioner learned the style of its references instead of learning to read the series. Reinforcement learning (RL) addresses both weaknesses. Trained with a verifiable reward, the captioner learns from its own samples rather than from demonstrations, so no paired data is needed (Lambert et al., 2024; Guo et al., 2025). RL also generalizes where SFT memorizes (Chu et al., 2025), in part because its updates stay close to the base model (Shenfeld et al., 2026). However, RL needs a good reward design, and the representative rewards used for image captioning do not work on time series (Figure 1). An LLM-as-judge reward (Figure 1a) takes an LLM’s grade of the caption as the reward. Because the grade is not verifiable, it is easily hacked (Xing et al., 2026). We observe that the captioner learns to game the grader instead of learning to describe the time series. An answerability reward (Figure 1b) asks whether questions about the time series can be answered from the caption alone (Xing et al., 2026). However, writing good questions and correct answers requires strong time series understanding, which current models largely lack (Zhou et al., 2026; Sen et al., 2025). A reward for time series captioning therefore has to satisfy three conditions. It must (i) resist reward hacking; (ii) demand only as much time series understanding as current LLMs or VLMs can supply; and (iii) reward important and distinctive features in time series. In this work, we propose LineupRL, a reinforcement learning with verifiable rewards (RLVR) pipeline built on caption-to-series identification, a reward that satisfies all three desiderata (Figure 1c). Our starting point is that a caption is grounded and specific if a reader who sees only the caption can pick the described time series out of close alternatives. LineupRL renders each time series as a line chart and trains a VLM captioner on it, so the input does not grow with the time series length. For each training time series it draws K − 1 distractors, which are real time series of the same length that are nearest to it in summary statistics. A frozen LLM verifier receives the caption and the K candidates as raw values and names the time series corresponding to the caption. The reward is the fraction of placements on which it names the true time series (Figure 1c), and each of these choices answers one of the three conditions. (i) The reward is an accuracy reward, the exact match of the verifier’s pick against the ground-truth index, rather than a judged score of the kind that is prone to reward hacking (Guo et al., 2025). (ii) The verifier solves a K-way multiple-choice question over printed numbers, which needs matching rather than question writing or grading, and a 14B off-the-shelf LLM supplies it. (iii) The K − 1 distractors are selected to concur with the true time series on general statistics, so the caption must describe the finer shape to earn the reward. Our contributions are as follows: • We trace ungrounded captioning to behavioral cloning of synthesized references, show why the two existing families of RL rewards for captioning fail on time series, and state the three conditions a verifiable reward for open-ended captioning must satisfy. • We propose LineupRL, whose reward is caption-to-series identification by a frozen verifier among statistic-matched distractors, which needs no reference captions.

Criterion 3: sufficiency as an information bottleneck. The last criterion asks how much of the series itself survives the encoding into language. We fit a decoder that reads the caption and predicts a numeric target on the chronologically earliest windows of each corpus, and report its error bS on the latest, so that no test target precedes any training window. Two targets are used: the horizon that follows the described window (forecasting) and the window itself (reconstruction). Because the series is never an input to the decoder, only what the caption states can lower bS. A low error is necessary but not sufficient, since a caption that only names a pattern common to the dataset would score well while identifying nothing, so we report it against three references: a text-blind predictor b that never reads a caption, the decoder refitted on an empty caption, and a detachment control in which the decoder is refitted with every window paired with another window’s caption. A caption counts as informative only if its error stays far below these. 

3 Experiments 

3.1 Experimental setup 

We train on 10 ,000 fragments sampled from TSFragment-600K (Ge et al., 2025) under a fixed seed, spread roughly evenly over the three lengths. Charts keep a handful of x and y tick labels, so the sampling positions and the value range remain readable, but carry no title, legend, series name, unit or per-point value labels, so the policy cannot rely on the dataset name or read values off the chart, and has to describe the shape. Every identification task uses K = 4 candidates. Criteria 1 and 2 are measured on BEDTime and CaTS-Bench; since the CaTS-Bench references name dates, places, domains and exact values that a bare chart does not carry, Criterion 1 on CaTS-Bench scores against references that Claude Opus 5 rewrote to remove them (Appendix B.1). Criterion 3 is measured on four forecasting corpora (ETTh2, ETTm2, Saugeen river flow and Australian electricity demand) under the chronological split of §2.3 (Appendix B). The evaluation data do not overlap the training data: no series of BEDTime, CaTS-Bench or the four forecasting corpora appears among the training fragments (Appendix B).

3.2 Main results 

Table 1 reports the performance of all models on the two benchmarks. Among the four models fine-tuned from the same initialization, LineupRL is best in all six columns. The two RL baselines show how much the reward design matters. The answerability reward ends up below the initialization it started from on all four identification columns. The LLM-as-judge reward loses less identification than the answerability reward on three of the four columns, but its entailment score collapses to 0.118 and 0.033 ,far beneath the base model. Against the 72B teacher itself, LineupRL is ahead in all six columns with 

1/24 of the parameters, which shows that an appropriate RL pipeline can raise a VLM’s time series captioning ability beyond what distillation offers. Another finding concerns the three text LLMs, which read the raw values instead of the chart. Their identification scores are far above every chart-reading model on both benchmarks, but this advantage does not carry over to entailment: even the largest of them trails LineupRL. Reading the raw values thus helps mainly with the fine detail that tells one series from another, and much less with describing the overall shape and events of the time series. 3.4 Case study 

Figure 4 shows the captions that the three RL reward designs produce for the same series, and it makes the weakness of both baseline rewards concrete. The answerability reward is limited by the model that sets its questions: posing and answering questions about a time series is far harder for an LLM or a VLM than doing so about a natural image. Only 28% of the questions the VLM wrote survive a check against the raw values with the answer the VLM gave (Appendix A.2), and most survivors merely ask which interval holds an extremum, so the captions this reward trains do not describe a time series well. The LLM-as-judge reward behaves as reported for image captioning, where an LLM judge is easy to hack: much of the caption is text about the caption itself, claiming that it is precise and complete, rather than about the time series. LineupRL, in contrast, describes the events of the series in order and gives the key values, and its caption is the shortest of the three. 

4 Related work 

Reinforcement learning with verifiable rewards. RLVR replaces the learned preference model of RLHF (Ouyang et al., 2022) with a programmatic check on the sampled output, optimized by critic-free group estimators such as GRPO (Shao et al., 2024) and RLOO (Kool et al., 2019; Ahmadian et al., 2024). RLVR applies most directly where a rule decides correctness outright (Shao et al., 2024; Guo et al., 2025; Zhang et al., 2026), and has since been extended to open-ended tasks by designing verifiers for them. Recent reward designs include rubrics decomposing a subjective answer into checkable criteria (Gunjal et al., 2026; Huang et al., 2025), outcome checks against an external environment (Jin et al., 2025; Zheng et al., 2025), and downstream utility (Berthon & van der Schaar, 2025).

Language models now write the descriptions themselves, either as dedicated captioners or as one capability of a time-series LLM (Trabelsi et al., 2025; Lin et al., 2026; Xie et al., 2024; Feng et al., 2026). Reinforcement learning has been applied to the time series domain where the task already supplies a verifiable target (Zhang et al., 2025; Fan et al., 2026; Liu et al., 2026; Hou et al., 2026). In our setting the description itself is the output, it is rewarded by whether a frozen verifier can identify the series from it alone, and no downstream task or label is involved. BEDTime (Sen et al., 2025) and CaTS-Bench (Zhou et al., 2026) benchmark how well current models caption a time series, and feature-level probes (Fons et al., 2024a; Merrill et al., 2024) test the same ability indirectly. 

Reinforcement learning for image captioning. Image captioning is closest to ours. Metric rewards score a sampled caption against human references and became standard with the self-critical baseline (Rennie et al., 2017; Liu et al., 2017). Human-feedback rewards replace the metric with ratings or written corrections (Fidler et al., 2017; Seo et al., 2020). Model-scored rewards employ a frozen cross-modal encoder to score the caption by its similarity to the image (Cho et al., 2022; Dzabraev et al., 2024) or by retrieval (Luo et al., 2018; Dessì et al., 2023; Chaffin et al., 2024). The retrieval line is the closest to ours in spirit: a listener must pick the described image among distractors. It relies on a retrieval model trained on paired image-text data, which does not exist for time series, and scores against distractors drawn at random or by image similarity. LineupRL replaces the listener with a frozen text LLM that reads the candidates as raw values, so no retrieval model is trained, draws the distractors from real series matched on summary statistics, and optimizes a VLM policy with verifiable rewards. 5 Conclusion 

We present LineupRL, a reinforcement learning framework that scores a time series caption by whether the series can be identified from it. By rewarding a caption for letting a frozen text-only verifier pick its series out of a set of close alternatives, LineupRL obtains its training signal from the data alone, without reference captions or a learned reward model. Across two captioning benchmarks and a caption-only forecasting and reconstruction evaluation, a 3B captioner trained this way surpasses SFT distillation, alternative RL rewards, and the 72 B model it could have been distilled from. Ablation studies validate the three choices the reward adds, the scale of the verifier, the number of candidates and the rule that draws the distractors, and show that the hard negatives give the better captioner on every criterion. Caption-to-series identification against close alternatives is thus a workable verifiable reward for continuous signals, where reference captions are scarce and LLM judges are easy to hack. Longer and multivariate series and captioners beyond VLMs are left to future work.

Ethics statement 

This work studies automated time series captioning using existing datasets and pretrained models. Generated captions may omit relevant events or misstate trends and numerical values. The identification reward measures whether a verifier can distinguish the target series from alternatives; it does not guarantee the correctness of every statement in a caption. Captions should therefore be checked against the underlying series before informing consequential decisions. Applications involving sensitive time series require appropriate privacy protections and evaluation in the intended domain.

A Implementation details 

A.1 Training LineupRL Policy optimization. The policy is Qwen2.5-VL-3B-Instruct, trained full-parameter with OpenRLHF on four GPUs (bf16, ZeRO-2, gradient checkpointing, FlashAttention-2). The advantage estimator is RLOO with G = 8 captions per chart at temperature 1.0, 32 charts per rollout, training batch size 128 ,learning rate 10 −6 with 20 warm-up steps, KL coefficient 0, reward normalization, generation capped at 1,024 tokens and no format reward. Four passes over the 9,500 training charts amount to about 1,200 

rollout steps. Every 50 steps we caption the 500 validation charts greedily and score them against their fixed distractors; validation reward peaks at step 550 (1.808 , verifier accuracy 90 .4% , against 1.172 and 

58 .6% at initialization) and is flat afterwards, so step 550 is the reported checkpoint. 

Reward. The frozen verifier is Qwen2.5-14B-Instruct in non-thinking mode, served alongside training under the prompt of Appendix D. The candidate set has K = 4 members printed as raw numbers at a shared precision, the reward scale is λ = 2 , so a caption earns one of {0, 0.5, 1, 1.5, 2}, and the overlap filter of §2.2 uses η = 0 .2. The same caption instruction (Appendix D) is used at rollout, validation and evaluation time. 

A.2 Baselines

RL, answerability. This baseline ports the CapRL recipe (Xing et al., 2026): a VLM writes multiple-choice questions about each training chart, and the reward is the accuracy of a frozen text-only answerer that sees the caption alone. Questions are written by qwen-vl-max reading the chart alone, at temper-ature 0.8, ten four-option questions with keys per chart under the instruction of Appendix D, which is CapRL’s question prompt adapted to a series: it asks for questions about the shape and where its features sit and forbids exact values, since a chart without value labels cannot support them. This yields 

95 ,329 questions over 9,985 charts. Because a VLM confidently answers its own questions wrongly, every question is then checked against the raw values: a text LLM (Qwen3.6-27B) drives a tool-calling loop that measures the series (extrema, segment statistics, feature detection) and the check itself is done by code, and the LLM only drives the tools. A.3 Ablation settings 

Each ablation setting changes one component of the recipe of Appendix A.1 and keeps everything else: the training data, the caption instruction, the policy-optimization hyperparameters and the Criterion 3 predictor. The reward-model ladder replaces the Qwen2.5-14B verifier of the reward with Qwen2.5-7B or Qwen2.5-3B, so on this ladder the model that scores the reward changes while the evaluation verifiers of Appendix B do not. The candidate ladder raises K from 4 to 6 or 8 with the same 14B verifier; the 

K − 1 distractors of a smaller K are a prefix of those of a larger one, so raising K adds candidates rather than exchanging them. The distractor-rule setting keeps K = 4 and the 14B verifier and replaces the nearest-neighbor rule by a uniform random draw from series of the same length. Every setting is a single training run at one seed (Appendix B.5) on the learning-rate schedule of LineupRL, so the settings of a ladder differ only in the component named above.

B.1 Criterion 1: entailment 

BEDTime supplies 2,000 series whose references are crowd-sourced or template-generated rather than LLM-written, giving |P| = 3 ,958 pairs; we use DeBERTa long-NLI at τ = 0 .5; an empty caption scores 

0.003 on this criterion and a reference scored against itself 1.000 . CaTS-Bench ships one reference per series but none of it is usable as written, since it states calendar dates, the place, the phenomenon and statistics of the source dataset, none of which our charts carry. The reported CaTS-Bench column therefore scores against rewritten references, described next. 

Rewriting the CaTS-Bench references. The 2,969 references were rewritten in two passes by Claude Opus 5, which saw the reference text only, never the series, the chart or any metadata; each pass was split into 30 slices of about 100 references, every slice under the identical instruction, and a rewrite that broke a rule on the first attempt was redone under the same instruction. Pass 1 removes the domain: the phenomenon or attribute name, places, units and currencies, comparisons to statistics outside the series, and calendar dates, which become relative positions (“about two-thirds of the way through”); it keeps every observation in its order and every number as written (Appendix D). Pass 2 starts from the output of pass 1 and removes every numeral and quantity, replacing each by the comparative fact it supported (“starting at 91.29 and reaching 105.27” becomes “starting low and ending higher”), so that the two reference sets differ only in the numerals (Appendix D). A script checks the result: after pass 1 every reference still contains a digit, after pass 2 none of the 2,969 does, and the mean length falls from 255 to 220 characters. We report the pass-2 references because 99 .9% of the pass-1 references state an exact quantity against 1.3% of BEDTime’s, and a caption written from a chart without value labels cannot entail an exact quantity however well it describes the shape: scored against pass 1, every chart captioner lands at the empty-caption floor, and the column would rank models by their access to the digits instead of by caption quality. 

B.2 Criterion 2: identification 

Evaluation series come from BEDTime ( 1,500 series from three of its four public subsets; sushi is excluded because its 2,048 -point series need roughly 57 k tokens for a single four-way option set, past the verifier’s context) and CaTS-Bench ( 2,969 series, lengths 8–150 ). We use their series but build our own protocol, because both benchmarks test what a model can do with the series itself, and we test what the caption preserves. We build the candidate sets ourselves, at K = 4 , as the three nearest neighbors in standardized (mean , std , min , max) space at the same length, whereas BEDTime’s own distractors are selected to be maximally dissimilar, which its authors describe as an upper bound on performance. Each identification number is the mean over two frozen verifiers that took no part in training any model, GLM-4-9B-0414 and Phi-4 (14B), each queried at all four gold positions (next paragraph). Per-position accuracy is reported as a control, and all captioners answer the same items. 

On the choice of the evaluation verifiers. The reward verifier is Qwen2.5-14B-Instruct, and cap →ser asks the question the reward asks. If the same model scored it, that column would only show how far the training objective generalizes to new series and candidate sets. The identification columns of Table 1 are therefore scored by two frozen LLMs that took no part in training any model, GLM-4-9B-0414 and Phi-4 (14B), and report their mean. Tables 7 and 8 give every captioner’s accuracy under five verifiers, the reward verifier, the two evaluation verifiers and two further LLMs, all under the same four-rotation protocol, so the reading of any one verifier can be checked against the others; Appendix C.1 says why the two further LLMs are not averaged into the main table. B.3 Criterion 3: caption-only forecasting and reconstruction 

The two targets share the decoder class and the windows, and in both cases the training loss and the reported metric are the same quantity, so no sampling noise enters the measurement. The split is chronological, so that adjacent windows cut at stride 1 cannot land on both sides of it. Each corpus is one univariate series whose rows are divided 60 /20 /20 in time order into a training, a validation and a test segment, following the ETT convention; a window is assigned to the segment that contains the first step of its forecasting target, which is the step after its lookback, and a window whose target runs past the end of the series is discarded. Every test target therefore starts later than every validation target, and every validation target later than every training target. The only contact between segments is at their boundary, exactly as in the standard forecasting protocol: the lookback of the earliest windows of a segment reaches back into the segment before it, and a training target runs at most 15 steps past the training segment. A test window never shares a step with any training window or training target, since the validation segment is longer than a window on every corpus. Reconstruction uses the same windows and the same assignment, so its test targets are the lookback windows of the test split, which reach into the validation segment but never into the training segment. The predictor is fitted on the training windows, early-stopped on the validation windows, and reported on the test windows only. Values are standardized with the statistics of the training segment, which makes bS dimensionless and comparable across datasets. The text-blind predictor b, the naive baseline of Table 2, is last-value persistence for forecasting and the mean training window for reconstruction; the empty-caption and detachment controls of §2.3 are the empty and shuffled caption rows of that table. On truncation: caption length differs across captioners by more than 2×, so the encoder’s default 256 -token cap would have cut every caption of the more verbose captioners and 21% of ours, and the ranking would then partly reflect length. Every run records its token statistics and aborts if any caption would be cut. 

Caption-only predictor. Windows are cut with a lookback of 128 and, for forecasting, a horizon of 

16 , at stride 1, assigned to the chronological 60 /20 /20 segments described above, and subsampled uniformly to 5,000 windows per dataset under a fixed data seed, which leaves about 3,000 training, 

1,000 validation and 1,000 test windows per corpus, the same windows for every captioner. Values are standardized with the statistics of the training segment. The decoder is google/embeddinggemma-300m 

with a LoRA adapter ( r = 16 ), mean-pooled and fed to an MLP head, trained for up to 30 epochs at learning rate 3 × 10 −4, keeping the epoch with the lowest validation MSE and stopping after 6 epochs without improvement; the test windows are scored once, with that epoch’s weights. The recipe is held fixed across captioners, so differences between cells come from the captions and not from tuning, and every cell is run with five training seeds. Table 2 reports the mean over those seeds; Table 6 repeats every cell with its standard deviation.

B.5 Statistical tests and noise Criteria 1 and 2. Both criteria are deterministic functions of a caption set: captions are decoded greedily, the candidate sets and the NLI threshold are fixed, and every captioner answers the same items. Tables 1, 7 and 8 therefore report each number once, as is standard for benchmark tables, and bold marks the best value of the fine-tuned group. The two statements about SFT in §1 are read off Table 1: on BEDTime entailment SFT scores 0.281 against 0.296 for the untuned initialization, so SFT does not raise entailment there, and we do not claim that it lowers it; on CaTS-Bench ser →cap SFT is below the initialization under both evaluation verifiers ( 0.598 against 0.680 under GLM-4-9B, 0.634 against 0.763 under Phi-4-14B). 

Significance of the Criterion 3 gaps. Bolding in Table 2 comes from a two-sided paired t-test across the 5 predictor seeds. Seeds are paired because a seed moves every captioner together: only the predictor’s training loop varies, so all 5 runs score identical windows against identical captions, and the paired difference removes the shared per-seed component. In each column we take the captioner with the lowest mean and bold it together with every captioner it cannot be separated from at p < 0.05 , so two bold entries mark a tie rather than a ranking. Table 5 lists the p-values of LineupRL against every other captioner. With n = 5 (df = 4 ) the test is lenient; it only tells which gaps are stable across seeds. Two further families of tests back the claims in §2.3. Every fine-tuned captioner beats both text-only controls in all eight dataset-study cells: across those 64 comparisons the weakest is p = 9 .8 × 10 −5.The two controls are in turn indistinguishable from each other in 7 of the 8 cells, the exception being forecasting on ETTm2 ( p = 0 .003 ), where the shuffled caption is 0.5% worse than no caption at all. A wrong caption is therefore about as useful as none, so the margin of a captioner over the controls measures series-specific content. 

Number of training runs. Every fine-tuned captioner in Tables 1 and 2 and every ablation setting of §3.3 is a single training run at one seed; only the Criterion 3 predictor is repeated over seeds, so the spreads in Table 2 are the predictor’s, not the captioner’s. Two noise scales help read the gaps. Scoring one checkpoint twice moves a Criterion 2 accuracy by about ±0.005 , and across the five predictor seeds the relative standard deviation of a Table 2 cell has median 3.6% and 90 th percentile 6.1% . LineupRL’s margin over the best fine-tuned baseline is between +0 .114 and +0 .253 on the six columns of Table 1 and above 9% of the error in six of the eight cells of Table 2; the two Saugeen cells are 5% (forecasting) and 1% (the reconstruction tie). The Criterion 3 tests compare the caption sets we obtained; they do not cover the variance of training itself, and a rerun of any model could move its numbers by more than the evaluation noise.
